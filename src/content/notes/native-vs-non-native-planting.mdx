<Callout type="note">
  **Quick answer.** For most gardens, the best approach isn't "natives only" or "anything goes". Research from the RHS and the Wildlife Gardening Forum suggests a mix works well for pollinators, with native plants as a strong backbone and carefully chosen non-natives extending the flowering season. Few gardening questions get answered with as much certainty, from both directions, as this one. One camp says only native plants truly help wildlife. The other says that's snobbery and a good ornamental border does just as well. The honest answer sits in between, and it's more useful than either slogan. This guide walks through what the evidence says, where "native" gets confusing, and how to put together a planting list that suits your own garden.

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture a typical scene. You've cleared a tired border, you're standing in the garden centre with a trolley, and a label says "native" on one plant and "pollinator-friendly" on the next. A neighbour has told you that anything non-native is useless to bees. Another told you their buddleia is covered in butterflies all August.

Who's right? Possibly both. A plant can be wonderful for wildlife without being native, and a native plant can still be a poor choice for your particular soil, light or exposure. Knowing what each category actually offers lets you choose with confidence rather than guilt, and it makes the planting list for your own space far easier to build.

## What Does "Native" Actually Mean?

The word shifts depending on where you are, and that's worth understanding before you compare anything.

- **Britain and Ireland:** "Native" generally means a plant that grew here naturally rather than being introduced by people. Britain and Ireland each have their own native flora, so a plant native to one isn't automatically native to the other.
- **United States:** The USDA's People's Garden guidance says a native plant should always be described with a geographic qualifier (for example, native to New England) and notes that only plants found in the country before European settlement count as native to the United States. A plant native to Texas isn't necessarily native to Vermont.
- **Australia:** A local council guide from Hornsby Shire in New South Wales explains the difference between *native* (from anywhere in Australia) and *indigenous* (local to a particular area). A plant from Western Australia is native to the country but not indigenous to Sydney.

The common thread is that "native" is always relative to a place. When you read advice, it's worth asking: native to where, exactly? A species that's native to the next county or state may still be a better local fit than a "native" from the other side of the country.

## What the Research Says About Wildlife Value

The most useful UK evidence comes from the Plants for Bugs project, a collaboration between the RHS and the Wildlife Gardening Forum. Here's how they describe it.

The RHS's own summary of the work, based on a four-year study at RHS Garden Wisley, recommends that gardeners wanting to support pollinators plant a mix of plants from different regions of the world, with an emphasis on plants native to Britain and the Northern Hemisphere. Southern Hemisphere plants were valuable mainly for extending flowering into late summer, and the advice highlights the importance of having something in flower every month, especially in early spring and late autumn when forage runs short.

The Wildlife Gardening Forum's page on the topic reaches a similar place. It says the evidence on natives alone "is not clear", reports that native plantings supported marginally more invertebrates overall, that near-native plants did nearly as well, and that the sheer amount of plant material mattered more than where plants came from. For pollinators, native and non-native Northern Hemisphere plants attracted comparable numbers.

There's an important caveat, though. Some insects, such as certain butterfly and moth caterpillars, depend on specific host plants, and those tend to be natives. If supporting those insects is a priority for you, that's a good reason to include native hosts.

In Ireland, the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan's planting guide takes a slightly stronger line, saying native plants are the better option, while accepting there's a place for non-native ornamental plants in the right settings, such as urban containers and coastal sites.

<Callout type="tip">
  Different organisations stress different things. That's not a contradiction. Natives are a reliable foundation, and the mix is what keeps something in flower for the longest stretch of the year.
</Callout>

## Climate Resilience and the Right Plant for Your Site

"Native" doesn't guarantee a plant will thrive in your garden, and "non-native" doesn't mean it will struggle. Local conditions matter just as much as origin.

A native woodland plant planted into a dry, sunny, sandy bed may sulk for years. A non-native from a similar climate may settle in happily. In both cases the question is the same: does this plant match your soil, your light, your exposure and your winter lows?

## Invasive Species: The One Firm Rule

This is where the debate stops being a matter of taste. Some non-native plants spread aggressively and damage natural habitats, and gardens are one of the main routes they escape through.

The RHS advises gardeners to avoid plants known to be invasive, particularly non-native aquatic species, and notes that in Great Britain it's an offence to plant or cause to grow in the wild any plant listed on Schedule 9 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. It also says not to put invasive plant material in home compost, council green waste or household waste bins.

Rules and lists differ by country and by state or region, so we won't try to give you a universal "banned" list here. Instead:

- **UK:** check the Schedule 9 list and the GB Non-Native Species Secretariat resources, and RHS advice.
- **Ireland:** check the National Biodiversity Data Centre and the relevant government agency for invasive species guidance.
- **US:** check your state's department of agriculture or natural resources, or your local extension service.
- **Australia:** check your state or territory's weed lists, since what's a problem in one area can be fine in another.

If you're unsure whether a plant is on a list, ask the nursery or look it up before you buy. It takes two minutes and saves a lot of trouble later.

## A Note on Cultivars

Cultivars are selected forms with different flower colours, sizes or habits. Are they as good for wildlife as the wild species?

It depends. An Oregon State University study of native plants and their cultivars found that nine of the eleven cultivars it examined supported different bee communities from their native counterparts, often a narrower range of bee types. The researchers linked this to changes in flower colour, size, bloom timing, nectar and pollen. But the study also noted that plant breeding has real benefits, such as disease resistance, and that some lightly modified cultivars performed similarly to the wild plants.

As a rule of thumb, we'd suggest favouring single-flowered, open forms with easy access to pollen and nectar, and keeping the wild species for plants where wildlife value is the main goal. This is a general principle, not a guarantee for any individual plant.

## Building a Mixed Planting Plan That Works

Here's a practical way to put all of this together.

1. **Start with structure.** Choose native trees, shrubs or a hedge as a backbone, matched to your soil and exposure.
2. **Add a seasonal layer.** Use perennials and bulbs, both native and non-native, so something flowers in every month.
3. **Fill the gaps.** Check early spring, late summer and autumn. These are typically where non-natives earn their place.
4. **Avoid invasives.** Cross-check any unfamiliar non-native against your national or local list.
5. **Keep it dense.** Both the RHS and the Wildlife Gardening Forum highlight the importance of the amount of planting, so a generous, layered border does more than a sparse one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are native plants always better for wildlife?

Not always. Native plants are a strong foundation and are essential for some specialist insects, but research suggests a mix of native and non-native plants can support pollinators well, especially when it keeps flowers available through more of the year.

### Is it wrong to plant non-native plants?

No. The key is to choose well and avoid known invasive species. Many non-native garden plants are valuable, particularly for extending the flowering season.

### What does "native" mean in the US and Australia?

In the US, the USDA says native plants are those present before European settlement and should be described with a region. In Australia, "native" means from anywhere in Australia, while "indigenous" means local to your particular area.

### Are cultivars as good for pollinators as wild plants?

Sometimes, but not always. Studies suggest that cultivars with heavily altered flowers can support fewer types of bees, while lightly modified forms can perform much like the wild plant.

### How do I know if a plant is invasive?

Check your country's official lists, such as Schedule 9 in Great Britain, or your state or local agency elsewhere, and ask your nursery. If in doubt, choose something else.
