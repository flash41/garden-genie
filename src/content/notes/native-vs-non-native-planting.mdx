<Callout type="note">
  **Quick answer.** Plant a mostly native backbone, then add a smaller number of carefully chosen non-natives to keep something in flower for more of the year. The RHS and the Wildlife Gardening Forum both point towards a mix, with native and northern hemisphere plants at its heart. Whatever you choose and wherever you garden, skip anything on your local invasive species list.
</Callout>

Few gardening questions are answered with as much certainty, from both directions, as this one. One camp says only native plants truly help wildlife. The other says that's snobbery and a good ornamental border does just as well. The useful answer sits between the two, and it changes a little depending on where you live.

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture a typical scene. You've cleared a tired border, you're standing in the garden centre with a trolley, and one label says "native" while the next says "pollinator-friendly". A neighbour has told you anything non-native is useless to bees. Another swears their buddleia is covered in butterflies every August.

Both could be right. A plant can be wonderful for wildlife without being native, and a native can still be a poor fit for your soil, light or exposure. Once you know what each category offers, you can choose with confidence rather than guilt, and building a planting list for your own space gets much easier.

## What Does "Native" Actually Mean?

It always means native to somewhere. The RHS describes a native plant as one that originated or arrived naturally in a particular place without human involvement. For the British Isles, that means plants that were here after the last ice age or have turned up unaided since. The USDA adds that the word should always carry a geographic qualifier, so a plant native to Texas isn't automatically native to Vermont.

So when you read planting advice, it pays to ask: native to where, exactly? A species from your own county or state is usually a better local fit than one that is "native" to the far side of the country.

## What Does the Research Say About Wildlife Value?

The best-known UK evidence comes from the Plants for Bugs project, a collaboration between the RHS and the Wildlife Gardening Forum. The RHS summary of the study at RHS Garden Wisley suggests that gardeners wanting to support pollinators should plant a mix of flowering plants from different parts of the world. It puts the emphasis on plants native to Britain and the northern hemisphere, with some southern hemisphere plants to stretch the flowering season into late summer.

The Wildlife Gardening Forum reaches a similar place. It says the evidence for natives alone is not clear, reports that native plantings supported marginally more invertebrates overall, that near-native plants did nearly as well, and that the amount of plant material mattered more than where plants came from.

There's one important caveat. Some insects, such as certain butterfly and moth caterpillars, depend on particular host plants, and those tend to be natives. If those species matter to you, include native hosts.

In Ireland, the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan takes a stronger line, with Dr Una Fitzpatrick saying native plants are the better option, while still recognising a place for non-native ornamentals in settings such as coastal sites and urban containers.

<Callout type="tip">
  Different organisations stress different things, and that isn't a contradiction. Natives are a reliable foundation. The mix is what keeps flowers available for the longest stretch of the year.
</Callout>

## How Do Natives and Non-Natives Compare?

| | Native plants | Well-chosen non-natives |
|---|---|---|
| **Wildlife value** | A strong foundation; essential for some specialist insects with specific host plants | Can offer comparable value to pollinators, especially northern hemisphere plants |
| **Flowering season** | Often concentrated in spring and summer | Can fill gaps in late summer, autumn and winter |
| **Suitability to your site** | Excellent if native to your region and matched to your soil | Varies; check hardiness and soil needs carefully |
| **Main risk** | A "native" that doesn't suit your soil or microclimate | A species that is invasive or unsuited to local conditions |
| **Best used for** | The backbone: hedges, trees, key structure | Seasonal extension, containers, colour |

## Will a Native Always Thrive in My Garden?

No. Local conditions matter as much as origin. A native woodland plant in a dry, sunny, sandy bed may sulk for years, while a non-native from a similar climate settles in happily. The question is always whether the plant suits your soil, light, exposure and winter lows.

That is why hardiness and aspect belong in the conversation. ## Which Non-Native Plants Should You Avoid?

Anything known to be invasive. This is where the debate stops being a matter of taste. Some non-native plants spread aggressively and damage natural habitats, and gardens are a major route by which they escape.

The RHS advises avoiding plants known to be invasive, particularly non-native aquatic plants. It notes that in Great Britain it is an offence to plant or cause to grow in the wild any plant listed on Schedule 9 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, and that invasive plant material shouldn't go into home compost, council green waste or household bins.

Lists differ by country, state and region, so check the local one (see the regional section below). If you're unsure about a plant, ask the nursery or look it up before you buy. It takes two minutes.

## Are Cultivars as Good as Wild Plants?

Not always. An Oregon State University study found that nine of the eleven cultivars it examined supported different bee communities from their native counterparts, often a narrower range. The researchers linked this to changes in flower colour, size, bloom timing, nectar and pollen. The study also noted that breeding brings real benefits, such as disease resistance, and that some lightly modified cultivars performed similarly to the wild plants.

As a rule of thumb, we've found it sensible to favour single-flowered, open forms with easy access to pollen and nectar, and to keep the wild species where wildlife value is your main goal. That is a general principle rather than a guarantee for any one plant.

## How Do You Build a Mixed Planting Plan?

1. **Start with structure.** Choose native trees, shrubs or a hedge as the backbone, matched to your soil and exposure.
2. **Add a seasonal layer.** Use perennials and bulbs, native and non-native, so something flowers in every month.
3. **Fill the gaps.** Check early spring, late summer and autumn, where non-natives often earn their place.
4. **Avoid invasives.** Cross-check any unfamiliar non-native against your national or local list.
5. **Keep it dense.** The RHS stresses abundance of flowers, so a generous, layered border does more than a sparse one.

## By region

### United Kingdom

"Native" here follows the RHS definition above: plants that arrived naturally after the last ice age. Britain's own flora differs from Ireland's, and within Britain, soil and climate vary enough that a local native often beats a distant one. For wildlife, the RHS and Wildlife Gardening Forum evidence supports a native and northern hemisphere core with a few extenders. For invasives, the GB Non-native Species Secretariat is the central information hub for England, Scotland and Wales, and the RHS invasive plants page explains the Schedule 9 offence. Take particular care with ponds, where non-native aquatic plants are a common escape route.

### Ireland

Ireland has its own native flora, so a plant native to Britain isn't automatically native here. The All-Ireland Pollinator Plan favours native species, with non-native ornamentals for specific situations such as coastal sites, acid or alkaline soils and urban containers. For invasives, Invasives.ie, run by the National Biodiversity Data Centre, lists regulated plants and offers "Be Plant Wise" guidance for gardeners and advice on pond plants. Check it before buying water plants in particular.

### United States

The USDA says native should always come with a geographic qualifier, and that only plants present before European settlement count as native to the United States. In practice, that means checking your own region, because a species native to one ecosystem can be out of place in another. The USDA-hosted National Invasive Species Information Center gathers federal, state and local sources, including state-specific listings, so start there and then check your state agency or local extension service. Regional hardiness varies enormously, so confirm your USDA zone before choosing any plant, native or not.

### Australia

Here the useful distinction is between native (from anywhere in Australia) and indigenous (local to a particular area). Hornsby Shire Council gives the example that a plant from Western Australia is native to the country but not indigenous to the Sydney area. For a garden that supports local wildlife, indigenous species are the closest match. Weeds Australia, managed by the Centre for Invasive Species Solutions, offers weed profiles and information on the 32 Weeds of National Significance, and your state or territory lists matter because a weed in one area can be fine in another.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are native plants always better for wildlife?

No, but they are the best foundation. They are essential for some specialist insects, yet research suggests a mix of native and non-native plants can support pollinators well when it keeps flowers available through more of the year.

### Is it wrong to plant non-native plants?

No. The key is to choose well and avoid known invasive species. Many non-native garden plants are valuable, particularly for extending the flowering season.

### What does "native" mean in the US and Australia?

In the US, native plants are those present before European settlement and should be described with a region. In Australia, native means from anywhere in Australia, while indigenous means local to your particular area.

### Are cultivars as good for pollinators as wild plants?

Sometimes, but not reliably. Cultivars with heavily altered flowers can support fewer types of bees, while lightly modified forms can perform much like the wild plant.

### How do I know if a plant is invasive?

Check your official local list, such as Schedule 9 in Great Britain, Invasives.ie in Ireland, your state agency in the US or your state weed list in Australia, and ask your nursery. If in doubt, choose something else.
