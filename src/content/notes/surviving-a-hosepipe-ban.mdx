As of this week, eight water companies across England and Wales have a Temporary Use Ban in force, covering Kent, Hampshire and the Isle of Wight, East Anglia, Devon, Cambridgeshire, London and the Thames Valley, Affinity's Central region, and parts of west Wales. Ireland went further: Uisce Éireann's Water Conservation Order, already covering Dublin and several counties, was extended nationwide from 24 July to 26 August. If you're gardening in the US or Australia, the hose isn't off limits by that name, but the underlying problem, and a lot of the solutions, are the same. We'll get to the terminology differences below.

What we've found, working with clients through dry spells for years, is that most of the panic around a hosepipe ban is misplaced. The things that actually die from three or four dry weeks are a much shorter list than people fear, and the things you can do without a hose make more difference than most people assume.

## What a Hosepipe Ban Actually Stops You Doing

The restriction is specific, not total. In the UK, a Temporary Use Ban prohibits using a hosepipe or sprinkler connected to the mains to water a garden, wash a car, fill a paddling pool, hot tub or ornamental pond, or clean paths and patios. It's enforceable in law, and breaching one can carry a fine of up to £1,000. In Ireland, the Water Conservation Order prohibits hosepipe use for the same activities, with a maximum fine of €5,000 for a breach.

What's still allowed, in both jurisdictions: a watering can, a bucket, or water from a butt. Hand-held containers are explicitly permitted under the Irish order, and a watering can filled from the water butt is unaffected by the UK ban too. Drip irrigation is generally permitted under most UK company rules, though it's worth checking your specific supplier's exemptions, since they vary. Blue badge holders, businesses that need water for their trade, and people on priority-services registers are typically exempt from the UK bans.

So the honest summary: you've lost the hose and the sprinkler. You haven't lost the ability to water anything, you've just lost the convenience of doing it without carrying a can.

## If You're Not in the UK or Ireland

The words are different but the mechanism is nearly identical. In the US, local authorities and water utilities issue "watering restrictions" in stages during drought, often limiting irrigation to specific days or hours and restricting overhead sprinklers while still permitting drip irrigation or hand watering with a shut-off nozzle. In Australia, there's no single national system, each state runs its own staged "water restrictions," typically numbered Stage 1 to Stage 4, escalating as reservoir levels drop; a hand-held hose fitted with a trigger nozzle is usually still allowed at the lower stages, with wider bans on hoses and lawn watering kicking in at Stage 3 and beyond. Whatever it's called locally, the priority order below and the water-saving habits further down apply regardless of jurisdiction.

## The Order Your Garden Actually Needs Water In

This is the bit that saves the most stress. Not everything in the garden needs saving equally, and treating a lawn with the same urgency as a young tree wastes water you don't have to spare.

**Newly planted trees, shrubs and anything still establishing.** Anything planted in the last one to two growing seasons has a root system too small to find its own water yet, and this is where a limited water budget should go first.

**Vegetables and anything you're growing to eat.** Unlike ornamental planting, edible crops can't simply shut down and wait out a dry spell without a real yield penalty, so they sit second in the priority order.

**Pots and containers.** Small containers dry out fastest of anything in the garden and have no reserve to draw on, so they need checking daily regardless of what else is going on.

**The lawn. Genuinely, last, or not at all.** This is the one people get wrong most often. According to the RHS, drought causes the top growth of a lawn to turn brown and die back, but in a well-established lawn the roots underneath are usually still alive and will send up fresh growth once rain returns. Resist the urge to water a parched lawn just to keep it green. It will very likely recover on its own, and the water is better spent on the tree that can't.

## What We've Found Works: Washing-Up Water, Water Butts and the Rest

Recycling household water is one of the most repeated pieces of drought advice, and it's mostly good advice, with one important distinction worth getting right.

**Washing-up water from the sink or bowl, yes, with care.** Water from hand-washing dishes in the sink can be reused on ornamental plants. The RHS's guidance is to apply it to the soil at the base of the plant rather than over the leaves, to avoid using it if it contains bleach or strong cleaning chemicals, and not to store it for more than 24 hours, since bacteria develop quickly. It's not recommended for edible crops, for hygiene reasons.

**Water straight from a dishwasher, no.** This is the distinction that gets lost in most "reuse your dishwater" advice. Water that's been through a dishwasher machine carries a much higher concentration of salts, grease and boron from the detergent and rinse cycle, and that combination can scorch plants and damage soil structure over repeated use, particularly in clay-heavy soil. If it came out of the dishwasher rather than the washing-up bowl, it goes down the drain, not on the border.

**A water butt, if you don't already have one, is the single best investment for the next ban.** Free rainwater with none of the greywater caveats. Even a modest butt fills fast off a garden shed roof and buys you weeks of watering-can capacity for containers and new planting.

**Mulch, now, not after.** A 75 to 100mm layer of mulch over bare soil around shrubs and borders slows evaporation dramatically. It's the cheapest thing on this list and the one people reach for last.

**Water early morning or late evening, never in the midday sun.** Less obvious than it sounds, since a huge proportion of water applied at midday is lost to evaporation before it ever reaches the roots. The same watering can, used at 7am instead of 1pm, does considerably more good.

## What Not to Bother With

In the interest of not sending you off to do unnecessary work: don't hand-water an established lawn, for the reasons above. Don't water shrubs and trees that are more than a couple of seasons established unless you're seeing genuine, sustained wilting rather than the ordinary midday droop many plants show in heat. And don't feel obliged to keep bedding and pots looking magazine-fresh through a ban; a slightly stressed display that survives the month beats a green one that used water better spent on the vegetable bed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use a hosepipe at all during a hosepipe ban?

Not connected to the mains supply for garden watering, car washing or filling pools. Water from a water butt applied by hosepipe is generally unaffected in the UK, since the restriction targets mains-fed use, but check your water company's specific wording. Drip irrigation is usually still permitted; hand-held hoses and sprinklers are what's actually banned.

### Will my lawn die if I don't water it during a ban?

Almost certainly not, if it's an established lawn. Based on RHS guidance, the browning you see is the grass going dormant to protect itself, and the roots typically survive to regrow once rain returns. Lawns can stay dormant for four to six weeks with little long-term damage. A genuinely dead lawn, one where the grass pulls up with no resistance and no green at the base, is the exception, and that needs reseeding rather than watering anyway.

### Is it safe to water vegetables with washing-up water?

We wouldn't recommend it, and neither does the RHS. Greywater from the sink is fine for ornamental plants applied at the soil, but the hygiene risk around edible crops means it's better to prioritise your limited clean water, or water-butt rainwater, for anything you're planning to eat.
