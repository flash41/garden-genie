<Callout type="note">
  **Quick answer.** Plenty will grow in a north-facing garden, but the right plants depend on *what kind* of shade you have: deep or dappled, dry or damp. Start by working out how many hours of direct sun a spot really gets and whether the soil stays moist, then choose shade-adapted structure plants, ground cover, flowers and ferns to match. In the southern hemisphere the logic flips: in Australia, it's the south-facing side that stays shady.
</Callout>

You've stood at the kitchen window and looked at it for a couple of seasons now: the bed along the back of the house where nothing seems to settle. The lavender sulked, the roses got leggy, and the one thing that thrived turned out to be moss. It's tempting to conclude that a north-facing garden is simply a lost cause. It isn't. It just needs a different shopping list, and a clearer picture of what your shade is actually doing.

---

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Most disappointing shady borders have the same story: sun-loving plants chosen for a spot that never gave them the light, or shade plants chosen without noticing that the soil was bone dry. Neither is a failing on your part. Garden centres tend to sort plants by season and colour, not by the conditions of your particular wall.

Put simply, a north-facing garden in the northern hemisphere sits on the side of the house away from the sun for much of the day, so the nearest border can stay in shade for long stretches. Many gardeners find the far end of the plot catches more light, and the picture changes through the year. That means "north-facing" is a starting clue, not a verdict. Read the light properly and you'll find you have more options than the label suggests.

If you're in Australia, flip this around. Because the sun tracks across the northern sky there, a north-facing garden is the bright, warm one, and the shady side of your house is the south. Everything below applies to *your* shady side; just swap the compass points. (The same applies to New Zealand and other southern-hemisphere gardens.)

## Step One: Read Your Shade

"Shade" is not one condition. The RHS describes several types, from light shade (open to the sky but screened from direct sun) and dappled shade (filtered through an open tree canopy), through partial or semi-shade (roughly 3 to 6 hours of direct sun) and moderate shade (around 2 to 3 hours), to deep shade with less than 2 hours, often beneath dense evergreens, according to the [RHS guide to shade gardening](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-design/shade-gardening). Each supports a different palette.

Two quick checks will tell you most of what you need:

- **Count the sun.** On a clear day, note when direct light reaches the spot, at about 9am, midday, 3pm and early evening. Photos work well. A border that looks gloomy at 8am may get a decent afternoon patch.
- **Check the moisture.** Dig down a spade's depth a couple of days after rain. If it's dusty, you have dry shade. If it's sticky and cool, you have damp shade.

The moisture point surprises people. The RHS notes that tree roots dry the soil heavily, whereas buildings cast shade without roots, so the soil beneath them dries out to a lesser extent. A wall or fence can still create a rain shadow, though. The RHS points out that north-facing walls can have dry soil at their base for exactly this reason, in its guide to [award-winning plants for shady north walls](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/trials-awards/using-award-winning-plants/10-award-winning-plants-for-shady-north-walls). Utah State University Extension adds that even beneath trees, soil can be surprisingly parched because canopies shed water outwards while the tree draws on what's underneath, in its [guide to landscaping in dry shade](https://extension.usu.edu/yardandgarden/research/landscaping-in-dry-shade).

Hardiness still matters, of course. Shade tends to be cooler and can hold frost longer, so check ratings against your climate using our guides to [RHS hardiness zones in the UK](/notes/what-planting-zone-is-the-uk-in), [Irish hardiness zones](/notes/plant-hardiness-zones-ireland-beginners-guide), and [how RHS ratings translate to USDA zones](/notes/rhs-hardiness-vs-usda-zones).

## Step Two: Choose Plants by Role

Rather than hunting for a list of "shade plants", think about what each part of the border needs to do. The names below are illustrations drawn from the sources we read and from common practice; always check the plant's label for the light and soil it prefers.

### Structure: the backbone

Evergreen or long-lasting shrubs and climbers give a shady corner bones through winter. For north-facing walls, the RHS highlights *Osmanthus delavayi*, *Itea ilicifolia*, *Azara serrata*, the climbing hydrangea (*Hydrangea petiolaris*), variegated ivy and *Euonymus fortunei* 'Emerald 'n' Gold', among others. Pale or variegated foliage is a useful trick, because it bounces what light there is around.

### Ground cover: closing the gaps

Bare soil in shade invites weeds and moss. The RHS suggests ground-covering perennials such as *Lamium maculatum* 'Beacon Silver' and sweet violet (*Viola odorata*) to fill space, in its [guide to shade planting for perennials and bulbs](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/profile?PID=100). Lady's mantle (*Alchemilla mollis*) also appears on both the RHS list and the Utah State dry-shade list, which is a good sign of adaptability.

### Flowers: yes, there are plenty

Shade isn't flowerless. The RHS lists perennials such as *Astrantia maxima*, hardy geraniums, hostas and Japanese anemones, and bulbs for early-season colour including snowdrops, winter aconites, *Cyclamen hederifolium*, *Scilla sibirica* and narcissi. Utah State's dry-shade list adds columbine, foamflower, coral bells (heuchera) and barrenwort (epimedium). If flowers matter to you, remember that many sun-lovers will survive in light shade but flower less, as the RHS notes.

### Ferns and foliage: the shade specialists

Ferns are the quiet heroes. According to the RHS, hart's tongue, painted lady and many *Dryopteris* ferns suit damp shade, while golden-scaled male fern, polypody and soft shield fern cope with dry shade. In very wet spots, shuttlecock fern, royal fern and sensitive fern are suggested. Ferns and hostas make a calm, layered scheme that suits a Japanese-inspired or woodland design just as well as a cottage one. We've explored how [plants earn their place in a temperate garden](/notes/plants-that-earn-their-keep-temperate-garden) if you want to build in some seasonal interest.

### Australian notes

Gardening Australia's Josh Byrne describes dry shade as particularly challenging, and shows a south-side bed where mother-in-law's tongue (*Sansevieria trifasciata*) and snake vine (*Hibbertia scandens*) cope with little added water, in the [Dealing with Shade](https://www.abc.net.au/gardening/how-to/dealing-with-shade/9431034) episode. The preparation advice there will sound familiar to northern gardeners: improve the soil with compost, water in thoroughly and mulch to hold moisture.

## Common Mistakes in Shady Gardens

- **Planting for the label, not the light.** "Partial shade" on a tag usually means a few hours of sun, not a permanently dim corner.
- **Forgetting the water.** Newly planted shade plants still need regular watering through their first season, especially in a rain shadow or under trees. The RHS warns against expecting sun-lovers to perform without adequate water.
- **Fighting the shade.** Trying to force a sunny border (roses, lavender, most herbs) into deep shade usually ends in thin growth and disappointment. Work with it instead.
- **Choosing dark foliage only.** A bed of all dark evergreens can feel heavy. Mix in pale leaves, silver markings, white flowers and variegation.
- **Too many single plants.** Repeat a few reliable plants in drifts rather than one of everything; it looks calmer and covers ground faster.
- **Overlooking edibles.** The RHS notes that Morello cherries and 'William's' pears can crop moderately in light shade and suit north and east-facing walls, so a shady wall needn't be purely ornamental.

## How the Light Changes Through the Year

A north-facing garden isn't static. In northern-hemisphere summer the sun rides high and swings wider, so spots that were gloomy in winter may pick up early or late sun. In winter the sun sits low and some borders may receive almost no direct light for weeks. Deciduous trees add another layer: spring bulbs flower in the bright window before the canopy closes, which is why woodland-style planting works so well.

Practical advice: assess your shade at more than one time of year, and ideally photograph it in spring, midsummer and late autumn. In the southern hemisphere the pattern mirrors, with the south side receiving the least winter sun. Tall neighbouring buildings, fences and evergreen hedges will complicate the picture whichever side of the equator you're on, which is why a short list of "north-facing plants" never quite fits every plot. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the best plants for a north-facing garden?

It depends on whether your shade is dry or damp and how deep it is. Good starting points include ferns, hostas, hardy geraniums, lady's mantle, spring performers such as snowdrops and cyclamen, and evergreen shrubs and climbers like *Osmanthus delavayi* and climbing hydrangea. Always match the plant to your soil moisture and hardiness zone.

### Does a north-facing garden get any sun at all?

Usually yes, though less than other aspects. It often depends on the time of year and the position within the plot: the part nearest the house may be shaded for long periods, while the far end can receive more light, especially in summer.

### Can you grow vegetables or fruit in a north-facing garden?

Some. The RHS suggests Morello cherries, 'William's' pears, cordon redcurrants and gooseberries can crop moderately in light shade on north or east-facing walls. Heavy-cropping sun-lovers like tomatoes are better in containers placed in the sunniest spot you have.

### What does "north-facing" mean in Australia or New Zealand?

The sun is in the northern sky there, so a north-facing garden is the sunny one, and the shady side is the south. Use this guide's advice for the south-facing side of your house.

### How do I improve dry shade?

Add compost to the soil before planting, water new plants in well, mulch to retain moisture and choose plants adapted to dry conditions, such as certain ferns (golden-scaled male fern, polypody) and ground cover such as lady's mantle.
