<Callout type="note">
  **Quick answer.** Clay soil does best with hydrangeas, hardy geraniums, hostas, roses and shrubs such as mahonia and viburnum; sandy soil does best with drought-loving plants such as lavender, rosemary, sedum and verbena. A five-minute squeeze test tells you which you have. Either way, the winning habit is the same: regular compost and mulch, not a one-off fix.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

You buy six lovely plants on a sunny Saturday, dig them in, and by midsummer half of them are sulking. A neighbour three doors down grows the very same plants without effort. Nothing is wrong with your gardening. The difference is usually underneath: the soil.

In clay, roots sit in cold, soggy ground all winter and then in cracked concrete all summer. In sand, water and feed disappear through the soil almost as fast as you apply them. Knowing which you have, before you spend money on plants, is one of the most useful five minutes you can give a new border.

---

## How Can You Tell Whether You Have Clay or Sand?

You don't need a lab. Take a handful of soil from a few places in the garden, from spade depth rather than the surface, and try these.

**The feel test.** Rub a pinch of moist soil between your fingers. The RHS describes sandy soil as gritty, with grains you can feel that fall through your fingers, and clay as smearing and sticky when wet, easily rolled into a long thin sausage ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/soil-composts-mulches/soil-types)).

**The ribbon (squeeze) test.** Moisten a ball of soil and squeeze it out between thumb and finger to form a ribbon. Colorado State University Extension suggests that a ribbon breaking before about an inch points to sandy soil, one reaching one to two inches suggests loam, and one holding beyond two inches suggests clay ([Colorado State University Extension](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/estimating-soil-texture-sandy-loamy-or-clayey/)).

**The jar test.** Fill a straight-sided jar about a third with soil, top up with water and a squirt of washing-up liquid, shake well and leave it to settle. Sand drops out within a minute or so, silt over the next hours, and clay stays suspended longest, so the layers show roughly what proportion of each you have. It's a rough guide rather than a lab result, but it makes a convincing picture.

**The chalk check.** If the soil fizzes when you drop it into vinegar, it contains free chalk (calcium carbonate), which makes it alkaline ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/soil-composts-mulches/soil-types)).

Most gardens are a blend, and soil can change from the front garden to the back, so test two or three spots. Our note on [where to begin a garden redesign](/notes/where-to-begin-garden-redesign) puts this kind of survey in the right order.

## Clay vs Sandy Soil: What Are the Real Differences?

| | Clay soil | Sandy soil |
|---|---|---|
| Feels like | Sticky, smooth, rolls into a sausage | Gritty, falls apart |
| Drainage | Slow, can waterlog | Fast, can dry out |
| Nutrients | Generally rich | Low, washed out by rain |
| Spring warm-up | Slow | Quick |
| Summer | Bakes hard and cracks | Dries out fast |
| Workability | Heavy, easily damaged when wet | Light and easy to dig |
| Biggest fix | Organic matter plus patience | Organic matter plus mulch |

The RHS puts the clay picture well: without some intervention it can be like concrete in summer and a sticky mess in winter, yet it is rich in nutrients and will happily support a considerable range of plants ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/for-places/clay-soils)). Sandy soil is the mirror image. It is easy to cultivate and warms up quickly in spring, but it dries out and loses nutrients to rain ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/for-places/sandy-soils)).

## How Does Each Soil Behave Through the Year?

**Clay.** Late winter and early spring are the tricky window. The ground is cold and slow to drain, and if you walk on or dig it while it's wet you can squash the structure out of it, which is hard to undo. By summer the same soil can crack open and set hard, although it does hold moisture better than sand in a dry spell ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/soil-composts-mulches/clay-soils)). If your problem is standing water rather than just heavy ground, see our guide to a [boggy, waterlogged garden](/notes/boggy-waterlogged-garden-what-to-do).

**Sand.** It's workable almost year-round and wakes up early, which suits spring bulbs and early veg. The trade-off arrives in summer, when it drains so quickly that watering barely lingers, and heavy rain in any season carries nutrients away. Sandy gardens are usually the first to show a dry spell, and our note on [reviving a garden after a hosepipe ban](/notes/reviving-your-garden-after-a-hosepipe-ban) is a useful companion.

## How Do You Improve Each One Without Wasting Effort?

The most reliable advice, from the RHS in the UK to university extension services in the US, is the same for both soils: add bulky organic matter, and keep adding it.

- **Clay:** Well-rotted manure, garden compost or composted bark opens up the structure over time. The RHS suggests a mulch of around 7.5cm of well-rotted organic matter in spring, and breaking up the base and sides of planting holes so they don't act like a bucket that fills with water ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/for-places/clay-soils)).
- **Sand:** Organic matter boosts the water- and nutrient-holding ability of sandy soil. Oregon State University Extension suggests laying one to three inches of compost over beds and working it into the top six to eight inches, then topping up each year ([Oregon State University Extension](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/news/add-organic-matter-improve-garden-soils)).
- **Mulch both.** After planting, a mulch holds moisture and keeps building the soil. On clay it also helps prevent summer cracking.
- **Choose composted material on sand.** Sandy soil can turn water-repellent. Burke's Backyard notes that well-composted material helps reverse this, where raw, uncomposted mulch can make it worse ([Burke's Backyard](https://www.burkesbackyard.com.au/fact-sheets/in-the-garden/gardening-tips-books-techniques-and-tools/adding-organic-matter-to-soil/)).

### Should you dig sand into clay?

No. It sounds logical, but it rarely works. The RHS notes that sand or grit only helps in very large volumes, on the order of hundreds of kilograms per square metre, which is impractical beyond a small patch ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/soil-composts-mulches/clay-soils)). Used in small amounts, it can make heavy soil set more firmly. Organic matter, raised beds and plants chosen for the conditions are far kinder to your back and your budget.

## Which Plants Cope Best?

Choosing to work with your soil is the quickest route to a border that looks settled in its first year.

**For clay.** The RHS lists Mahonia, Hydrangea, hardy geraniums, *Sorbus aria* 'Lutescens' and *Viburnum* × *bodnantense* 'Dawn' among its top performers, with hostas, hellebores, crocosmia, alliums, narcissi, birch and hawthorn also suiting it ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/for-places/clay-soils)). Roses generally do well on clay too ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/soil-composts-mulches/clay-soils)). Plant in late winter or early spring when you can, and mound woody plants slightly so water drains away from the stems.

**For sand.** The RHS suggests drought-resistant plants that need little watering once established: lavender, *Geranium* 'Rozanne', *Verbena bonariensis*, buddleja, rosemary, thyme, cistus, sedum, eryngium, achillea, kniphofia and agapanthus all feature ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/for-places/sandy-soils)). Mediterranean-style planting is a natural fit.

**For chalky or alkaline soil.** It is often shallow, free-draining and lower in fertility, and it won't support ericaceous (acid-loving) plants such as rhododendrons and most heathers ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/soil-composts-mulches/soil-types)). If you love them, grow them in pots of ericaceous compost.

**A word on pH.** Soil acidity or alkalinity affects which plants thrive, and a cheap home test kit is enough to tell you whether you lean acid or alkaline. Climate matters alongside soil, so it's worth pairing this with our guide to [RHS hardiness versus USDA zones](/notes/rhs-hardiness-vs-usda-zones).

## By region

Soil is one of the most local things about a garden, and the mix of clay, sand and chalk changes a lot across our three main markets. These are broad patterns only; your own squeeze test always beats a map.

### England and Wales

The Soilscapes map, developed by Cranfield University and sponsored by Defra, sorts the soils of England and Wales into 28 types ([LandIS Soilscapes](https://www.landis.org.uk/soilscapes/)). Three matter most to gardeners. There are loamy and clayey soils with impeded drainage, described as moderately fertile and often seasonally wet, where land is tile drained and under grass or arable ([LandIS Soilscapes](https://www.landis.org.uk/soilsguide/soilscapes.cfm?ssid=18)). There are freely draining sandy soils such as those of the Breckland, which drain well but are vulnerable to nutrient leaching ([LandIS Soilscapes](https://www.landis.org.uk/soilsguide/soilscapes.cfm?ssid=11)). And there are shallow lime-rich soils over chalk or limestone ([LandIS Soilscapes](https://www.landis.org.uk/soilscapes/)). The practical lesson: even within one county you can move between all three, so check your own plot, and use the Soilscapes viewer to see which type your postcode area falls in. If you're on the heavy, seasonally wet type, follow the clay advice above and avoid working the ground when it is saturated.

### Scotland and Northern Ireland

Soilscapes covers England and Wales only, so the map above won't tell you much here ([LandIS Soilscapes](https://www.landis.org.uk/soilsguide/soilscapes.cfm?ssid=11)). The squeeze test does the same job, and the RHS plant lists for clay and sand still apply to most gardens, provided you choose varieties hardy for your exposure.

### Ireland

Ireland's soils lean towards the heavy end. Ireland's State of the Environment report puts well- and moderately drained soils (podzolics, brown earths, podzols and rendzinas) at 55% of the country, poorly drained mineral soils (gleys) at 24.5%, and organic soils (peats and lithosols) at 18% ([EPA Ireland](https://epawebapp.epa.ie/ebooks/soe2024/151/)). Teagasc describes gleys as poorly drained mineral soils with poor physical properties, in contrast with well-drained brown earths ([Teagasc](https://teagasc.ie/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Teagasc_soil_groups_and_forestry.pdf)). So roughly a quarter of the country is prone to wetness, and a heavy, slow-draining garden is a very normal thing to find. Teagasc's national soil survey maps are available if you want to look up your county ([Teagasc](https://www.teagasc.ie/environment/soil/soil-maps/)), though they are described as a reconnaissance overview rather than a plot-by-plot guide. For a wet garden, raised beds and the plants in the clay list above are the safest starting point.

### United States

Soil changes sharply by region, so a "clay" or "sandy" label means different things from coast to coast. In the Southeast, Georgia's Piedmont is famous for its red clay, the result of long weathering that leaves iron oxides behind, while the Coastal Plain contains red clay, sands and gravels, with the Lower Coastal Plain notably sandy and nutrient-poor ([New Georgia Encyclopedia](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/geography-environment/soils/)). Gardeners in the Southeast are advised to check texture, compaction and drainage after a soaking rain rather than assuming a region-wide pattern ([Annie's Annuals](https://www.anniesannuals.com/blogs/news/southeast-gardening-regions)). In practice that means Piedmont gardeners should lean on the clay plant list and generous mulch, and coastal-plain gardeners on the sandy list plus compost every year. We haven't yet sourced detailed guidance for other US regions, so use the squeeze test and your state's cooperative extension service.

### Australia

Both extremes are common, and the fixes are local. In Western Australia, the Water Corporation advises that water sitting on top of the soil rather than soaking in is a sign of water repellence, and recommends mixing clay and compost into the top layer when planting and spreading a chunky mulch around 10cm deep ([Water Corporation](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/garden/creating-a-waterwise-garden-in-5-steps)). On clay, Australian gardeners are often pointed to gypsum, which one nursery guide says can help break up clay and make it easier to dig, though results vary by soil, and to digging wide, shallow planting holes backfilled with the original soil. That guide lists eucalypts, callistemons, acacias and grevilleas among natives that tolerate clay ([The Seed Collection](https://www.theseedcollection.com.au/the-essential-guide-to-gardening-on-clay-soil)). Check with your state or local council for advice that matches your own soil.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if my soil is clay or sand?

Rub a moist pinch between your fingers: clay feels sticky and smooth and rolls into a sausage, sand feels gritty and crumbles. A squeezed ribbon that holds beyond about two inches suggests clay, while one that breaks before an inch suggests sand.

### Can I make clay soil better by adding sand?

No. Sand only helps in very large quantities, and small amounts can leave heavy soil firmer. Compost, well-rotted manure and mulch, applied regularly, are more effective.

### What is the best plant for clay soil?

There isn't a single winner, but hardy geraniums, hydrangeas, mahonia and *Viburnum* × *bodnantense* 'Dawn' are among the strongest performers according to the RHS, and roses generally do well too.

### How do I stop sandy soil drying out?

Add composted organic matter each year and mulch after planting. Choose drought-tolerant plants such as lavender, rosemary and sedum, and use well-composted material rather than raw mulch if the soil has become water-repellent.

### Does soil pH matter as much as soil type?

Both matter. Type affects drainage and nutrients, while pH affects which plants can take up what they need. A simple test kit will tell you whether acid-lovers like rhododendrons are an option or best kept in pots.
