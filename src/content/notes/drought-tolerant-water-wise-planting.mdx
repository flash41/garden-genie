<Callout type="note">
  **Quick answer.** A water-wise garden isn't built from one miracle plant. It comes from improving the soil, mulching it, choosing plants suited to dry conditions, planting at the right time of year, and watering deeply but rarely while roots establish. Do those five things and most gardens need far less from the hose, and cope better when restrictions arrive.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture the second week of a dry spell. The hosepipe ban, or watering restrictions if you're in Australia or parts of the US, has just been announced. You walk the borders at dusk with a watering can, choosing which plants to save and which to quietly give up on. The new shrubs go first in the queue, then the pots, then you glance at the hydrangea and wince.

That evening routine is exhausting, and it's mostly avoidable. Gardens that cope with dry weather were usually set up to, long before the weather turned. If you're in the middle of a restriction right now, our note on [surviving a hosepipe ban](/notes/surviving-a-hosepipe-ban) is the place to start, and [reviving your garden afterwards](/notes/reviving-your-garden-after-a-hosepipe-ban) covers what to do once the rain returns. This note is the longer-term companion: how to design the garden so you're not the one doing the rescuing.

## Start Below the Surface: Soil and Mulch

Water-wise gardening begins with the ground, not the plants. The RHS advises working organic matter, such as home-made compost, into planting areas, because it helps soil hold moisture and drain better whatever the soil type, from sandy to clay ([RHS, Drought-resistant gardening](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/profile?PID=396)). That's good news if you're unsure what you've got. Our guide to [clay versus sandy soil](/notes/clay-soil-vs-sandy-soil-what-will-grow) explains how each behaves, but compost helps both.

Mulch is the second layer. A covering over the soil slows evaporation and keeps roots cooler, and it suppresses weeds that would otherwise compete for the same water. Denver Water's xeriscape principles list soil improvement and mulch as two of their seven core steps ([Denver Water, Seven Principles of Water Conserving Landscapes](https://landscape-water-conservation.extension.org/seven-principles-of-water-conserving-landscapes-xeriscapes)), and the Melbourne International Flower & Garden Show suggests a layer of roughly 5 to 10cm ([Melbourne International Flower & Garden Show, 10 Ways To Save Water In Your Garden](https://melbflowershow.com.au/10-ways-to-save-water-in-your-garden/)).

Two practical details are worth knowing:

- **Match the mulch to the plant.** Moisture-loving shrubs and perennials suit organic mulch such as compost or bark. Mediterranean plants that dislike wet crowns tend to do better with a gravel mulch, which the RHS suggests at around 5 to 7.5cm.
- **Time it well.** The RHS recommends mulching newly planted trees and shrubs after a thorough watering, but not piling mulch onto established plants in the middle of a dry spell, as it can stop what little rain falls from getting through. Wait for the autumn rains ([RHS, How to protect your garden in a heatwave and drought](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-inspiration/get-gardening/what-not-to-do-in-a-heatwave-and-drought)).

## Choose Plants That Are Built for Dry Weather

Plants that cope with dry conditions tend to share a few traits, and once you know them you can spot candidates in any nursery. The RHS points to silver or grey foliage, deep root systems, and origins in dry regions as the common features ([RHS, Drought-resistant gardening](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/profile?PID=396)). In practice we've found it helps to think in four groups:

- **Mediterranean-type shrubs and herbs.** Lavender, rosemary, sage, thyme, cistus and santolina enjoy sun and sharp drainage.
- **Silver and grey foliage.** Artemisia, stachys and eryngium reflect light and lose less water.
- **Deep-rooted perennials.** Echinacea, sedum, achillea and many salvias reach water that shallower plants can't.
- **Ornamental grasses.** Stipa, festuca and miscanthus add movement and generally ask for little once settled.

In Australia and the warmer US states, the same logic points to local natives and plants from similar climates, such as grevilleas, correa or kangaroo paw in Australian gardens, or salvias and agastache in many American ones. Pick whatever suits your climate zone. If you're not sure where you sit, our guide to [low maintenance garden ideas by climate](/notes/low-maintenance-garden-ideas-by-climate) breaks down what tends to thrive where.

Grouping matters too. Planting thirsty and drought-tolerant plants side by side means either the thirsty ones suffer or the tough ones get overwatered. Denver Water calls this hydrozoning: put plants with similar needs together, with the thirstiest closest to the house or a water source. And remember that even a sun-loving plant struggles in the wrong spot. Our notes on [right plant, right place](/notes/right-plant-right-place-garden-orientation) and [microclimates](/notes/microclimates-in-your-garden) will help you find the hot, free-draining corners that suit these plants best.

## Plant at the Right Time, Then Water Smart

Drought-tolerant doesn't mean water-free from day one. The RHS is clear that new trees, shrubs and perennials should be watered regularly through their first year, even when labelled drought-tolerant, to encourage deep roots, and that larger specimens may need care for up to three years ([RHS, Drought-resistant gardening](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/profile?PID=396)).

That's why timing matters so much. The RHS suggests planting in March, as soil warms, or in September while the soil is still warm. In the northern hemisphere, that means early autumn is a sweet spot: warm soil, cooler air, and rain on the way. Roots get a head start before the next summer tests them. In Australia, the same logic applies in reverse, so aim for the cooler, wetter months of autumn and early spring rather than the height of summer. The calendar differs, the principle doesn't.

Once plants are in, a few habits make water go further:

- **Water deeply, less often.** Soak the root zone rather than sprinkling the surface, so roots chase moisture downwards.
- **Aim at the base.** The RHS advises directing water at the soil around the plant, ideally in the cooler morning or evening, rather than over the foliage.
- **Use targeted irrigation.** The Melbourne show's list includes drip irrigation, buried clay pots (ollas) and wicking beds, all of which deliver water where roots need it with less lost to evaporation.
- **Reduce as plants settle.** After the establishment period, many dry-garden plants need little or nothing beyond rainfall.

## Catch the Rain You Do Get

Water butts turn roof runoff into a free (and, in most places, restriction-friendly) supply. A single downpipe connection is enough to start, and the RHS suggests using an online calculator to estimate how much your roof could collect before the wet season begins, and cleaning out stored water and containers in late summer ready for autumn rain. Rules differ by country and water company, so check yours: in the UK and Ireland, water butts fed from roofs are commonly allowed during hosepipe bans, while Australian states and US municipalities have their own tank and rain barrel rules.

If space allows, a larger tank, or several butts linked together, makes a real difference through a dry spell. Gravity is the only pump you need for a watering can, and rainwater is kinder to ericaceous plants than hard tap water.

## Rethink the Lawn

Lawns are where many gardens lose the most water, but the picture isn't as bleak as it looks. The RHS notes that established lawns rarely need watering and usually green up quickly once rain returns. So the first step is simply to stop watering and let it go golden. Avoid heavy foot traffic on dormant grass, and think about aerating and feeding in autumn.

If you'd rather change the lawn itself, Denver Water's principles include replacing turf with lower-water alternatives. A few approaches we see working:

- **Shrink it.** A smaller, better-shaped lawn with deeper borders gives you more room for resilient planting.
- **Swap sections for groundcover.** Creeping thyme, chamomile and similar low plants take light foot traffic in sunny spots.
- **Gravel garden.** A free-draining bed of drought-tolerant plants set in gravel can look wonderful and needs little water.
- **Meadow-style planting.** Longer grass with wildflowers is lower input and good for pollinators.

Which one suits you depends on how you use the space, how much sun it gets, and what's already around it. That's exactly the kind of decision that's easier to make when you can see it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best time of year to plant drought-tolerant plants?

In the northern hemisphere, the RHS suggests March or September, while soil is warm enough for roots to grow and before the toughest weather arrives. Early autumn is particularly good. In the southern hemisphere, aim for the cooler, wetter months of autumn and early spring.

### Do drought-tolerant plants need watering at all?

Yes, at the start. Even plants labelled drought-tolerant need regular watering through their first year while roots establish, and larger shrubs and trees may need it for longer. After that, many manage on rainfall alone.

### Should I water my lawn during a hosepipe ban or water restriction?

Generally no. Established lawns usually recover once rain returns, so there's no need to use limited water on them. Prioritise new plantings, vegetables and containers instead.

### Is gravel or organic mulch better for a dry garden?

It depends on the plants. Organic mulches such as compost or bark suit most borders. Gravel suits Mediterranean plants that dislike damp around their crowns. Either way, keep the layer in place, but avoid adding more organic mulch to established plants in the middle of a drought.

### Will a water-wise garden look dry and brown?

Not at all. Silver foliage, grasses, structural perennials and good mulch can look lush and layered, and a well-planned dry garden often looks better in summer than a thirsty one struggling through it.
