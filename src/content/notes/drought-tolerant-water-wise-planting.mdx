<Callout type="note">
  **Quick answer.** Plant for drought by doing five things in order: add organic matter to the soil, mulch it, choose plants suited to dry conditions, plant in early autumn or spring, and water deeply but rarely for the first year or so. There is no single miracle plant. Do these five and most gardens need far less from the hose, and cope calmly when restrictions arrive.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture the second week of a dry spell. The hosepipe ban, or watering restrictions if you're in Australia or parts of the US, has just been announced. You walk the borders at dusk with a watering can, choosing which plants to save and which to quietly give up on. The new shrubs go first in the queue, then the pots, then you glance at the hydrangea and wince.

That evening routine is exhausting, and it's mostly avoidable. Gardens that cope with dry weather were usually set up to, long before the weather turned. If you're in the middle of a restriction right now, our note on [surviving a hosepipe ban](/notes/surviving-a-hosepipe-ban) is the place to start, and [reviving your garden afterwards](/notes/reviving-your-garden-after-a-hosepipe-ban) covers what to do once the rain returns. This note is the longer-term companion: how to design the garden so you're not the one doing the rescuing.

## What Should You Do to the Soil First?

Water-wise gardening begins with the ground, not the plants. The RHS advises working organic matter, such as home-made compost, into planting areas, because it helps soil hold moisture and drain better whatever the soil type, from sandy to clay ([RHS, Drought-resistant gardening](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/profile?PID=396)). Our guide to [clay versus sandy soil](/notes/clay-soil-vs-sandy-soil-what-will-grow) explains how each behaves, but compost helps both.

One caution from drier climates: Colorado State University's horticulturist suggests aiming for only a modest amount of organic matter, around 3 to 5 per cent, for xeric plants, as over-enriching can do harm ([Colorado State University, The 7 principles of xeriscape landscaping](https://engagement.source.colostate.edu/7-principles-of-xeriscaping-horticulture-expert-explains/)). So improve the soil, but don't bury Mediterranean-type plants in rich compost.

## Does Mulch Really Make a Difference?

Yes. A covering over the soil slows evaporation, keeps roots cooler, and suppresses weeds that would compete for the same water. Mulching is one of the seven core steps in the Denver Water xeriscape principles ([Seven Principles of Water Conserving Landscapes](https://landscape-water-conservation.extension.org/seven-principles-of-water-conserving-landscapes-xeriscapes)). The RHS suggests at least 5cm, and the Melbourne International Flower & Garden Show suggests a layer of roughly 5 to 10cm ([Melbourne International Flower & Garden Show, 10 Ways To Save Water In Your Garden](https://melbflowershow.com.au/10-ways-to-save-water-in-your-garden/)).

Two practical details are worth knowing:

- **Match the mulch to the plant.** Moisture-loving shrubs and perennials suit organic mulch such as compost or bark. The RHS suggests gravel for Mediterranean plants that dislike wet crowns.
- **Time it well.** The RHS recommends mulching newly planted trees and shrubs after a thorough watering, but not piling mulch onto established plants in the middle of a dry spell, as it can stop what little rain falls from getting through. Wait for the autumn rains ([RHS, How to protect your garden in a heatwave and drought](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-inspiration/get-gardening/what-not-to-do-in-a-heatwave-and-drought)).

## Which Plants Cope Best With Dry Weather?

Plants that cope with dry conditions tend to share a few traits, and once you know them you can spot candidates in any nursery. The RHS points to silver or grey foliage, deep root systems, and origins in dry regions as the common features. Its simplest rule is to choose plants suited to your conditions, because those won't need summer watering once settled ([RHS, Drought-resistant gardening](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/profile?PID=396)). In practice we've found it helps to think in four groups:

- **Mediterranean-type shrubs and herbs.** Lavender, rosemary, sage, thyme, cistus and santolina enjoy sun and sharp drainage.
- **Silver and grey foliage.** Artemisia, stachys and eryngium reflect light and lose less water.
- **Deep-rooted perennials.** Echinacea, sedum, achillea and many salvias reach water that shallower plants can't.
- **Ornamental grasses.** Stipa, festuca and miscanthus add movement and generally ask for little once settled.

Grouping matters too. Planting thirsty and drought-tolerant plants side by side means either the thirsty ones suffer or the tough ones get overwatered. The xeriscape principles call this hydrozoning: put plants with similar needs together, with the thirstiest closest to the house or a water source. And even a sun-loving plant struggles in the wrong spot. Our notes on [right plant, right place](/notes/right-plant-right-place-garden-orientation) and [microclimates](/notes/microclimates-in-your-garden) will help you find the hot, free-draining corners that suit these plants best.

## When Should You Plant, and How Often Do You Water?

Drought-tolerant doesn't mean water-free from day one. The RHS is clear that new trees, shrubs and perennials should be watered regularly through their first year, even when labelled drought-tolerant, to encourage deep roots, and that larger specimens may need care for longer ([RHS, Drought-resistant gardening](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/profile?PID=396)).

That's why timing matters. The RHS suggests planting in March, as soil warms, or in September while the soil is still warm. Early autumn is a sweet spot: warm soil, cooler air, and rain on the way, so roots get a head start before the next summer tests them. In Australia the logic runs in reverse, so aim for the cooler, wetter months of autumn and early spring rather than the height of summer.

Once plants are in, a few habits make water go further:

- **Water deeply, less often.** Soak the root zone rather than sprinkling the surface, so roots chase moisture downwards. Colorado State's guidance makes the same point.
- **Aim at the base.** Direct water at the soil around the plant, ideally in the cooler morning or evening, rather than over the foliage.
- **Use targeted irrigation.** The Melbourne show's list includes drip irrigation, buried clay pots (ollas) and wicking beds, all of which deliver water to roots with less lost to evaporation.
- **Taper off.** After the establishment period, many dry-garden plants need little or nothing beyond rainfall.

## Is It Worth Catching Rainwater?

Water butts turn roof runoff into a free supply. A single downpipe connection is enough to start. The Environment Agency notes that water butts are a way to collect rainwater, and that you can still carry water in containers to plants during a hosepipe ban ([Environment Agency, Why do we have temporary use (hosepipe) bans during a drought?](https://environmentagency.blog.gov.uk/2025/06/12/why-do-we-have-hosepipe-bans-during-a-drought/)). If space allows, a larger tank, or several butts linked together, makes a real difference through a dry spell. Rules differ by country and supplier, so check yours.

## Should You Keep the Lawn?

Lawns are where many gardens lose the most water, but the picture isn't as bleak as it looks. The RHS notes that established lawns rarely need watering and usually green up quickly once rain returns, so the first step is simply to stop watering and let it go golden.

If you'd rather change the lawn itself, limiting turf is one of the seven xeriscape principles, with drought-tolerant perennials and xeric grasses as the replacement (Colorado State University). A few approaches we see working:

- **Shrink it.** A smaller, better-shaped lawn with deeper borders gives you more room for resilient planting.
- **Swap sections for groundcover.** Creeping thyme, chamomile and similar low plants take light foot traffic in sunny spots.
- **Gravel garden.** A free-draining bed of drought-tolerant plants set in gravel can look wonderful and needs little water.
- **Meadow-style planting.** Longer grass with wildflowers is lower input and good for pollinators.

Which one suits you depends on how you use the space, how much sun it gets, and what's already around it.

## By region

The five steps hold everywhere. What changes is how dry it gets, what the rules are, and which plants are easiest to find.

### UK

England has had the most recent trouble. The Environment Agency's account of the 2025 to 2026 drought says it affected eight English water companies, including Thames, Southern, South East Water and Yorkshire ([Environment Agency](https://environmentagency.blog.gov.uk/2025/06/12/why-do-we-have-hosepipe-bans-during-a-drought/)). By November 2025 the National Drought Group reported that the South East and East of England had received the least rain, that Sussex had been declared in drought, and that Yorkshire, Thames, South East and Southern still had hosepipe bans in place ([Water Magazine, National Drought Group warns drought likely to continue into 2026](https://www.watermagazine.co.uk/2025/11/03/national-drought-group-warns-drought-likely-to-continue-into-2026-due-to-record-dry-weather/)). If you garden in the south-east, assume restrictions are a realistic possibility and lean towards the Mediterranean and deep-rooted groups above. Bans are imposed by your water company, so check its rules for your postcode, and note that the RHS's March or September planting windows are written for UK conditions.

### Ireland

Ireland is used to rain, which made the summer of 2026 a useful reminder. Uisce Éireann put a Water Conservation Order in place nationwide from 24 July 2026, asking customers to use a watering can instead of a hose. It was narrowed to 13 counties on 26 August and lifted nationwide on 16 September ([Uisce Éireann, Water Conservation Orders](https://www.water.ie/water-support-hub)). The practical lesson is that even in a wet climate a few dry weeks can leave new plantings exposed, so mulch and a water butt earn their place here too.

### US

In the semi-arid West, the xeriscape approach was developed to cope with limited water. Denver Water's seven principles cover planning, soil, efficient irrigation, hydrozoning, mulch, turf alternatives and maintenance. Colorado State University adds Colorado-specific advice: the usual problem is too little water rather than poor drainage, so avoid adding sand or gravel to the soil, and use gravel or wood chips as a surface mulch instead. It also reports that established xeric plants often manage on natural precipitation alone, and suggests choosing for site suitability rather than native status alone ([Colorado State University](https://engagement.source.colostate.edu/7-principles-of-xeriscaping-horticulture-expert-explains/)). Elsewhere in the US, local extension services and water utilities publish plant lists for your own zone.

### Australia

Restrictions are common and tightly enforced. In Western Australia, the Water Corporation allows sprinkler watering only before 9am or after 6pm on your rostered watering days, which depend on your street number and postcode, with on-the-spot fines for breaches ([Water Corporation, Check your watering days](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/waterwise/watering-days/check-your-watering-days)). It also promotes native plants, noting that plants suited to the local climate need less water, and lists WA natives such as banksias, grevilleas and eremophilas ([Water Corporation, A-Z guide to native plants](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/garden/a-z-guide-to-wa-native-plants)). Other states have their own rules, so check your own supplier. For methods, the Melbourne show's tips on mulch depth, drip irrigation and wicking beds apply well across southern Australia.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best time of year to plant drought-tolerant plants?

In the UK, the RHS suggests March or September, while soil is warm enough for roots to grow and before the toughest weather arrives. Early autumn is particularly good. In Australia, aim for the cooler, wetter months of autumn and early spring.

### Do drought-tolerant plants need watering at all?

Yes, at the start. Even plants labelled drought-tolerant need regular watering through their first year while roots establish, and larger shrubs and trees may need it for longer. After that, many manage on rainfall alone.

### Should I water my lawn during a hosepipe ban or water restriction?

No. Established lawns usually recover once rain returns, so spend limited water on new plantings, vegetables and containers instead.

### Is gravel or organic mulch better for a dry garden?

Use organic mulch such as compost or bark for most borders, and gravel for Mediterranean plants that dislike damp around their crowns. Keep the layer at least 5cm deep, but avoid adding more organic mulch to established plants in the middle of a drought.

### Will a water-wise garden look dry and brown?

No. Silver foliage, grasses, structural perennials and good mulch can look lush and layered, and a well-planned dry garden often looks better in summer than a thirsty one struggling through it.
