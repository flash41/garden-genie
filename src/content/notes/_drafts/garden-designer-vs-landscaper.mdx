The first time a lot of homeowners think seriously about renovating the garden, the next thought is "I'll call a landscaper." That is a perfectly reasonable starting point, in the same way "I'll call a builder" is a perfectly reasonable starting point when thinking about an extension. The problem is that both starting points skip the same step, and the step they skip is where most of the value of the project actually lives.

A garden designer designs the space. A landscaper builds it. Those are different jobs, done by different people, with different training, different costs, and very different points in the project where they earn their fee. Hiring the wrong one first, or assuming one is the other, is the most expensive small mistake a homeowner can make at the start of a renovation. So before any conversations begin, it is worth being clear about who does what.

---

## What a Garden Designer Actually Does

A garden designer is the person who works out what the garden should be. They sit with a brief, walk a site, take measurements, think about light, drainage, sightlines, how the family actually uses the space, and turn all of that into a plan. The plan is a document. It specifies the layout, the materials, the planting, the phasing, and often the lighting and irrigation. It is the thing that lets everyone else do their job properly.

[Gardens Illustrated explains](https://www.gardensillustrated.com/garden-advice/what-is-landscape-designer-garden-architect) that a garden designer is typically trained in horticulture and general design principles, and that their work sits on a smaller, more residential scale than a landscape architect. They are interpreting a client's idea of the garden (the messy, fragmented one most clients arrive with) and translating it into something buildable.

The output of a good garden designer is not a mood board. It is a set of drawings detailed enough that a landscaper can quote against them line by line, and a planting plan specific enough that the right plants end up in the right places. Without that document, every quote a homeowner receives is a guess.

---

## What a Landscaper Actually Does

A landscaper builds the garden. They are contractors, in the proper sense of the word. They lay paving, build walls, install fencing, dig drainage, lay turf, put in irrigation, plant the planting plan, and finish the space to a usable standard. Good landscapers are skilled craftspeople. They know which way the slope wants to fall, why that retaining wall needs a weep hole, and how long the cement should cure before anyone walks on it.

[The National Design Academy notes](https://www.nda.ac.uk/blog/landscape-design-garden-design/) that a landscaper's job requires a working knowledge of hard landscaping materials, construction methods, fencing, paving, decking, walls, and structures like pergolas. It is, by some distance, the most physical of the three roles people are trying to choose between when they start a project.

A landscaper is not paid to redesign the space mid-build. They are paid to build what the plan specifies. If the plan is vague, or if there is no plan at all, then either the landscaper improvises (which becomes the design by default) or they keep asking the client to make decisions on site, which is the slowest and most expensive way to design a garden.

## Where the Confusion Comes From

A lot of firms offer "design and build" as a single service. That is genuinely useful for some projects. But it muddies the language, because the same person is occupying both roles, and the homeowner often does not realise that two distinct disciplines are being applied.

[House Designer notes](https://housedesigner.com/garden-designer-vs-landscaper/) that some landscapers do offer layout suggestions and some do this well, particularly on smaller or more straightforward projects. But their core expertise lies in building and installation rather than spatial planning, visual composition, or long-term garden strategy. That is not a criticism. It is just the truth about which problems each profession spends ten years getting good at.

It is worth understanding that there is also a third category lurking nearby. A landscape architect is the most heavily qualified of the three, usually working on commercial projects or larger residential ones, often with a degree-level qualification recognised by a national body. For most homeowners renovating a back garden, a landscape architect is overkill and a garden designer is the right level. It is helpful to know the word exists, though, because some firms use "landscape architect" loosely on their websites when what they mean is "garden designer who is good at drainage."

---

## Which One to Hire First, and When

The order matters more than the choice. A garden designer is the first person to engage on most projects, because their output is what every other professional, including the landscaper, will need to quote against.

The exception is small or simple work. A new fence on its own. A patio replacement with no change of level. Replacing a tired flowerbed with new planting in the same footprint. For projects of that scale, a good landscaper can usually quote from a site visit alone, and the cost of a designer would be disproportionate to the work. [As Holland Landscapes puts it](https://hollandscapes.co.uk/do-i-need-a-landscaper-and-a-garden-designer/), most landscapers will be able to quote for jobs that involve one or two elements without the help of a garden designer, but for complex jobs with multiple design elements or changes of level, a landscaper genuinely needs professionally drawn plans to work from.

The honest test is this. If three different landscapers were asked to walk the same garden and quote for the same scope of work, would they all come back with the same understanding of what is being built? If yes, the project is straightforward enough to go straight to a landscaper. If the answer is "probably not," there is a design problem hiding inside the project, and a designer needs to solve it first. Otherwise the homeowner ends up comparing three loose estimates, none of which is actually a like-for-like quote.

## How the Money Splits

Design fees and build costs are different lines on a budget, and confusing them is one of the easier ways to overshoot.

A garden designer's fee is typically charged either as a fixed fee for the design package or as a percentage of the construction cost, usually somewhere between 8 and 20 percent depending on the complexity of the brief and the scale of the work. The fee covers the plans, the planting design, the materials specification, and a defined amount of project support during construction.

The build cost is everything else. Hardscaping, plants, soil, irrigation, lighting, labour, plant hire, waste removal, any trades the landscaper needs to subcontract (electricians for outdoor lighting, for example). The build is by some distance the larger of the two figures. A €15,000 garden might have €2,000 of design fees inside it and €13,000 of build, depending on the scope and how detailed the design package is.

Where homeowners get into trouble is when they treat the design fee as optional. The design pays for itself within the build, because the build costs less when the landscaper is quoting against a precise plan instead of a verbal description and a hand-wave at the back fence.

## The Self-Implementing Gardener Exception

There is a third path that a lot of homeowners are quietly interested in. They want the design, but they want to do some or all of the build themselves. Sometimes that is the budget talking. Sometimes it is genuine enjoyment of the work. Either way, it changes the brief.

For a self-implementing homeowner, the landscaper part of the equation is replaced (in whole or in part) by a series of weekends, a hired skip, a borrowed van, and a friend or two who owe a favour. What stays critical is the design. Without a clear plan, a self-build project tends to drift, with new ideas added every weekend until the original design is unrecognisable and the budget has crept up by a third.

A garden designer can produce a plan that a homeowner can build from. Some designers will go further: setting out the plants on site when they arrive from the nursery, leaving the homeowner to do the physical planting. This split (professional design, self-build implementation) is a sensible middle path for a lot of households, especially when the work can be phased across a season.

---

## How to Brief Whichever One Is Hired First

Once the choice is made, the brief is the next thing. A landscaper hired without a designer needs to know: the scope of work, the materials preferences, the must-haves, the must-not-haves, the budget, and any constraints that affect the build (access, neighbours, existing features that need protecting). A designer hired before a landscaper needs all of the above, plus a sense of how the homeowner actually wants to use the garden, what styles they are drawn to, and what level of involvement they want in the build itself.

Both benefit from photographs. Both benefit from a rough sketch. Both benefit from the homeowner being honest about budget. The biggest single difference is that a landscaper is being asked "can you build this?" while a designer is being asked "what should this be?" Different questions, different conversations, different scopes of work.

---

## The Practical Takeaway

The choice between garden designer and landscaper is not really a choice between two competing options. It is a question of sequence. For anything beyond a small, well-defined piece of work, a designer goes first and a landscaper follows. For small, well-defined work, a landscaper alone is usually enough. The thing not to do is assume the landscaper will quietly absorb the design work as part of the build, because the result is rarely what the homeowner imagined when they first walked into the back garden and decided something needed to be done.
