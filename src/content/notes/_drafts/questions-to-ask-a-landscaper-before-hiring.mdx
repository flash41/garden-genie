When a homeowner finally decides to get something done about the garden, the next step is instinctively to call a professional, describe the space vaguely as "a bit overgrown, needs a bit of a lift," and then sit through a consultation trying to answer questions they have never really thought about. What style is the brief? Is low maintenance a priority? Is there a budget in mind? How much sun does the back of the garden get in the afternoon?

A lot of people leave that first meeting feeling a bit embarrassed, as though they showed up to a job interview without having prepared. So the more prepared a homeowner is before that conversation, the better the outcome, not because landscapers expect their clients to have all the answers, but because those answers shape a lot of the project. A vague brief tends to produce a vague result, vaguely near the actual requirements.

---

## What Should the Garden Actually Do?

This might sound obvious but a lot of homeowners skip it. A good garden is not just a visual space, it has to be a functional one. Somewhere to host people in the summer? A quiet spot to sit in the morning? A safe space for children to play? A low-maintenance patch that can be more or less ignored for a few weeks at a time?

The function shapes everything. A garden designed for entertaining needs different hardscaping than one designed for wildlife habitat. Getting clear on how the space will be used is the single most valuable thing any homeowner can do before a professional turns up.

A short list is enough. Even three points will do. Something along the lines of "somewhere to sit outside in the morning, room for eight people in summer, no more than two hours a week on maintenance" gives a landscaper genuine information to work from. It is also, incidentally, the thing most people only manage to articulate halfway through the consultation itself, by which point everyone is already discussing paving.

## What Is the Real Budget, and What Does It Cover?

This is where a lot of garden projects go sideways. Homeowners often arrive at an initial consultation with a number in their head that covers the idea of the project rather than the reality of it. [According to the Irish League of Credit Unions](https://www.creditunion.ie/blog/the-complete-cost-of-a-garden-renovation/), average suburban gardens with lawn, raised beds, borders and patio typically land in the €8,000 to €14,000 range when design and build are combined, while smaller front gardens or compact plots tend to fall between €4,000 and €8,000 for a complete redesign and build.

In the US, [HomeGuide reports](https://homeguide.com/costs/landscaping-cost) that full landscaping projects typically range from $5,000 to $25,000 depending on scale, with smaller refresh projects starting around $3,000. In Australia, full backyard transformations commonly fall between AUD $10,000 and $40,000 for mid-sized suburban properties.

Design fees alone, separate from any construction, tend to sit between 8 and 20 percent of the total project cost. That is before materials, plants, drainage, lighting, or any of the other extras that have a habit of appearing once work begins. A hard ceiling is essential, along with clarity on whether the figure includes tax.

If the figure is significantly below what projects of similar scope typically cost, it is better to know that before falling in love with a proposal. A good landscaper will help phase the work realistically, but they need an honest number to work with.

## What Style Is the Homeowner Drawn To, and Can They Show It?

"Natural but tidy" means different things to different people. So does "modern" or "cottage-y" or "low maintenance but nice-looking."

Twenty minutes spent saving images of gardens that genuinely appeal, before any consultation, pays off significantly. Not gardens that look impressive in photographs, but gardens a homeowner actually wants to sit in. There is a difference, and it is usually the difference between a Pinterest board and a real life. A pattern usually emerges quickly: structured formal layouts with clipped hedging and clean lines, or informal planting with billowing grasses and loose borders. Neither is wrong, but they are very different projects.

[Houzz notes](https://www.houzz.com/magazine/10-questions-to-ask-a-landscape-designer-stsetivw-vs~78310749) that a garden designer's role is much like that of an architect for an interior. They are interpreting a brief and translating it into a buildable plan. The clearer and more visual the input, the more accurately the output reflects what the client actually wants. A short folder of saved images, brought to the first meeting, does more for the brief than an hour of describing the same things in words.

## Are the Site Conditions Understood?

A homeowner does not need to be a horticulturalist, but a basic grasp of the site is essential before anyone starts planning plants or paving.

Which direction does the garden face? A south-facing garden in the northern hemisphere, or a north-facing one in the southern, gets significantly more sun than its opposite, and that changes the entire planting palette. Standing in the garden at midday and noting where the sun falls is enough to start with. Is the soil heavy clay that waterlogs in winter, or sandy and free-draining? The difference matters enormously for what will thrive and what will struggle. [Landscaping professionals emphasise](https://gardeningservicesdundalk.com/landscaper-near-me/) that local knowledge of soil types and weather conditions is one of the most critical things to look for in a designer.

Does water sit on the surface after heavy rain? Are there low spots that flood? In climates with significant rainfall, drainage is rarely an afterthought. And what is staying? Mature trees, walls, outbuildings. If a mature tree is going nowhere, a designer needs to know that from day one, not after presenting a plan that quietly assumes it isn't there.

## Design-Only, or Design and Build?

This is a distinction that catches a lot of homeowners off guard. [As Houzz explains](https://www.houzz.ie/magazine/how-much-would-it-cost-to-redesign-my-garden-stsetivw-vs~127833468), a garden designer is a design specialist. They produce the plan, the planting scheme, and the materials specification. They are not necessarily the person who builds the garden. A landscaper is the contractor who builds it, usually from a plan. Some firms offer both as a combined service, others specialise in one or the other.

Hiring a designer separately from a landscaper means managing two relationships and two sets of fees. Hiring a design-and-build firm means one point of contact, but potentially less independence in choosing materials or contractors. Neither model is better in absolute terms. But knowing which model suits the project, and which one the person across the table actually offers, is essential before any conversation about scope or cost begins.

## Is the Plan for Self-Implementation, or Full Handover?

This question matters more than a lot of people realise, and it changes the shape of the brief entirely. A lot of homeowners want a professional design, a proper plan with plant species, material specifications, phasing and costings, but want to do some or all of the implementation themselves. Sometimes that is to save money, sometimes because the work itself is part of the appeal. Either way, it should be said upfront.

[Kerr & Kerr Landscaping notes](https://kerrandkerrlandscaping.com/season/10-questions-to-ask-when-hiring-a-landscaper/) that subcontracting and project management structures vary significantly between firms. A homeowner planning to be hands-on needs a designer who will produce plans detailed enough to work from. Some designers will produce a technical specification and planting plan, set out the plants when they arrive on site, and leave the physical planting to the homeowner. Others prefer to be involved throughout.

For a self-implementing gardener, the most valuable thing to walk away with is not inspiration. It is a clear Garden Action Plan. One that specifies exactly what to plant, where, in what order, and what the materials will cost. That is the document that turns a designer's ideas into something a homeowner can actually execute, in phases, over a series of weekends, at their own pace.

---

## What Is the Honest Maintenance Commitment?

This question rewards bluntness. A lot of homeowners overestimate how much time they will spend maintaining a garden once the initial enthusiasm has worn off. The gap between "a beautiful cottage garden" as an aspiration and "thirty minutes a week, realistically" as a commitment is where a lot of landscaping projects quietly fail in year two.

[Rosehill Palms Landscaping advises](https://www.rosehillpalms.com/what-questions-should-i-ask-before-hiring-a-landscaper) asking landscapers specifically which of their suggested plants require low maintenance versus high maintenance, and why. Not all "low maintenance" planting actually is, depending on soil and climate. An honest answer about time, ability and enthusiasm changes the design entirely. A garden built for someone who can give it two hours a month looks completely different to one designed for someone who will be out there every weekend. Both can be beautiful. But only one will still look beautiful in three years.

---

## The Preparation That Actually Matters

A lot of these questions have one thing in common. They are asking a homeowner to understand the space before handing it to someone else. The direction it faces. The light it gets. The way water moves through it. The way it will be used. What can realistically be committed to.

That is not a lot to ask. But it is, genuinely, most of the work. Once those questions can be answered, with photos, a rough sketch, and a few saved images of the aesthetic in mind, a homeowner is ready to have a productive conversation with any designer or contractor. And the first meeting no longer feels like an interview the candidate forgot to prepare for.
