Most people don't actually need convincing that DIY is cheaper, or that hiring someone is faster. They know both of those things already. What's harder is knowing which one is right for their specific project, because the honest answer depends on a handful of factors that rarely get named plainly. Here's the framework we'd actually use.

---

## Start With What's Structural and What's Not

The single biggest factor isn't budget or time, it's whether the project involves structural hard landscaping, retaining walls, significant level changes, drainage, anything load-bearing or water-related, or whether it's mostly planting, surface-level tidying, and smaller builds like raised beds or a pergola.

Structural work carries real consequences when it goes wrong. A retaining wall that fails, or drainage that wasn't properly graded, can cost far more to fix than it would have cost to do properly the first time. Planting and surface-level work carries much lower stakes. A border that doesn't work can be redone next season for the price of a few plants. If your project sits mostly in the first category, that's a strong signal toward at least the structural portion being professionally done, even if you handle everything else yourself.

---

## Be Honest About the Hours, Not Just the Weekends

DIY garden projects rarely fail because the work was too hard. They fail because the time required was underestimated, and momentum died somewhere around week three. Our note on [a realistic weekend-by-weekend DIY plan](/notes/diy-garden-makeover-plan) covers the actual sequencing, but the honest time commitment behind it is worth naming here: a meaningful garden renovation done yourself is closer to two or three months of regular weekends than the "one big push" most people picture when they start.

That's not a reason not to do it. It's a reason to be honest about whether that time genuinely exists in your calendar over the coming season, not just whether the idea of it sounds appealing on a Saturday morning.

---

## Run the Numbers Both Ways, Properly

The cost comparison people run in their heads is usually incomplete. Materials plus your own time versus materials plus labour looks like an easy win for DIY, and on materials and labour alone, it usually is. Labour typically accounts for 40 to 65% of a professional project's total cost, so removing it is a real saving.

What gets left out is everything else DIY brings with it: tools you don't already own, skip hire, delivery costs when you can't buy in the same bulk a contractor can, and the cost of redoing anything that goes wrong the first time. None of that is a reason to default to hiring, but it's worth pricing honestly before comparing the two totals, because the gap is usually smaller than the first back-of-envelope number suggests. Our [full cost breakdown](/notes/garden-design-cost-breakdown-2026) and the [worked example of where a typical budget goes](/notes/where-the-money-goes-in-a-garden-renovation) are both useful for pricing your specific project properly before you compare.

## The Middle Path Most People Miss

It's rarely all one or the other. The most common approach that actually works well: hire out the structural, technical, or physically demanding parts, groundwork, drainage, larger hard landscaping, and do the planting, finishing, and lighter builds yourself. This gets the consequential parts of the project done properly by someone who does it daily, while keeping the parts where a mistake is cheap to fix, and often the most enjoyable parts, in your own hands.

This also tends to be the best use of a limited budget. Concentrate professional spend on the work where a mistake is expensive, and put your own time into the work where a mistake just costs a season.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it actually cheaper to do a garden yourself?

On materials and labour alone, usually yes, since labour is typically 40 to 65% of a professional project's cost. Once tools, skip hire, and the cost of fixing mistakes are priced in honestly, the gap is often smaller than it first looks.

### What parts of a garden project should never be DIY?

Anything structural or water-related carries the highest stakes if it goes wrong: retaining walls, significant level changes, and drainage. These are the parts most worth hiring out even in an otherwise DIY project.

### How long does a DIY garden renovation actually take?

Realistically closer to two or three months of regular weekends for a meaningful project, not a single big push. Underestimating this is the most common reason DIY projects stall.

### Can I mix DIY and hiring a landscaper on the same project?

Yes, and it's often the best approach. Hiring out the structural or technical work while doing the planting and finishing yourself keeps consequential decisions in professional hands and lower-stakes work in your own.
