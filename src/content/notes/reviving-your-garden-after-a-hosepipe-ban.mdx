There's a particular moment that comes after a hosepipe ban lifts or the rain finally arrives: you walk out expecting relief, and instead you're looking at a garden that seems worse than it did mid-drought. Brown patches, crisp leaf edges, a lawn that looks more like straw matting than grass. Before you reach for the credit card or the spade, it's worth working out what's actually dead and what's simply been holding its breath.

What we've found doing this kind of recovery work is that gardeners consistently overestimate the damage. Established plants are tougher than a few dry weeks suggest, and the biggest mistake people make after a ban lifts is overcorrecting, flooding everything with water at once, when a slower, more targeted approach recovers more of the garden.

## Dormant or Dead? The Pull Test

Before anything else, work out what you're actually dealing with. For lawn grass specifically, tug gently at a brown patch. If it comes away with real resistance and you can see any green at the base, it's dormant, not dead, and it doesn't need reseeding, just time. If it lifts away easily with no resistance at all, that patch has died and will need reseeding rather than watering.

For shrubs and perennials, scratch a small section of bark or stem with a fingernail. Green or pale tissue underneath means the plant is alive even if the top growth looks finished. Brown, dry tissue all the way through means that section, or the whole plant, has died back and won't recover on its own.

## Give It Time Before You Panic

This is the step most people skip. According to RHS guidance, a well-established lawn's roots typically survive drought even as the leaf blades die back, and the recommended approach is to get the living portions back onto a consistent watering pattern and then allow two to three weeks before doing anything about bare patches. Reseeding or replacing too early means working against a plant that was already on its way back, and wastes both the seed and the effort.

The same patience applies to shrubs and perennials showing drought stress rather than outright death. A stressed but living plant often needs several weeks of normal conditions to show its real recovery, not several days.

## What to Reseed and What to Leave Alone

Once you've done the pull test and given the living sections their two to three weeks, you'll have a much shorter list of genuinely dead patches than you expected mid-drought.

For lawn areas that are confirmed dead, re-sow with a drought-tolerant seed mix rather than replacing like-for-like, since the point of this exercise is a lawn that copes better next time, not a repeat of the same vulnerability. If the drought exposed genuinely poor soil underneath, and it often does, this is also the moment to work organic matter into the area before reseeding, since a lawn on improved soil holds moisture for considerably longer than one on compacted, thin topsoil.

Leave anything showing green at the base alone. It's tempting to tidy up brown growth immediately, but cutting back too hard on a recovering plant removes the leaf area it needs to photosynthesise its way back to full health.

## Trees, Shrubs and Perennials: What Actually Needs Attention

Once restrictions have lifted, the instinct is to make up for lost time with frequent light watering. What actually helps more is the opposite: a smaller number of deep, thorough waterings that reach the full root zone, rather than a daily splash that only wets the surface and encourages roots to stay shallow, which leaves the plant more vulnerable to the next dry spell.

Newly planted trees and shrubs, the ones that needed priority watering during the ban itself, are the ones most worth this attention now. Established, previously healthy shrubs that pulled through the dry spell mostly just need a return to normal care and a fresh layer of mulch to help the soil hold on to whatever rain does fall.

## Building a Garden That Copes Better Next Time

If this is the second or third dry spell you've gardened through, it's worth treating it as a signal rather than a one-off. The plants that sailed through with barely a wilt are worth noting and repeating; the ones that needed daily intervention to survive are worth reconsidering at the next planting opportunity. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it take a lawn to recover after a drought?

Based on RHS guidance, allow two to three weeks after rain returns before judging whether a patch needs reseeding. Most of an established lawn's browning is dormancy rather than death, and the roots typically send up fresh growth in that window without any intervention beyond normal watering.

### Should I water everything heavily as soon as a hosepipe ban ends?

No, and this is the most common overcorrection we see. A small number of deep waterings that reach the full root zone does more good than frequent light watering, which mostly wets the surface and trains roots to stay shallow. Save the heaviest watering for newly planted trees, shrubs and anything still establishing.

### How do I know if a plant is dead or just drought-stressed?

Scratch a small area of stem or bark with a fingernail. Green or pale tissue underneath means it's alive, even if everything above ground looks finished. Dry, brown tissue all the way through means that part has died and won't recover, though the rest of the plant may still be fine.
