<Callout type="note">
**Quick answer.** Yes, raised beds are better in most small and problem gardens: if your soil is wet, compacted or contaminated, or you want less bending, build them. If your soil is decent, your plot is large, your budget is tight or your summers are hot and dry, in-ground beds are usually the better choice. Plenty of good gardens use both.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

You've decided you want somewhere to grow things, whether that's vegetables, cut flowers or a proper herb patch. Then comes the first fork in the road. Do you build a row of tidy timber boxes, or simply dig into the lawn?

Both look good in a magazine. Only one will suit your soil, your back and your budget. The difference tends to show up months later, when a raised bed has dried out in a warm week, or an in-ground bed is sitting in a puddle in February. Choosing well at the start saves a lot of second-guessing.

---

## What Are the Main Differences at a Glance?

| | Raised beds | In-ground beds |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront cost | Higher: frames, fill and often delivery | Lower: mostly digging and soil improvement |
| Drainage | Better in wet or compacted sites | Depends entirely on your existing soil |
| Watering | Dry out faster, so need checking more often | Hold moisture longer, especially with mulch |
| Accessibility | Less bending; height can be tailored | Needs kneeling or bending |
| Poor or contaminated soil | Lets you sidestep it with imported soil | You work with, and improve, what you have |
| Root depth | Limited by the bed's depth unless open to the soil below | Roots can go as deep as the soil allows |
| Longevity | Frames eventually need repair or replacing | No structure to wear out |
| Best for | Small spaces, problem sites, mobility needs | Larger plots, tight budgets, dry sites |

## Which Is Cheaper, and Which Handles Poor Soil Better?

**Cost.** In-ground beds are almost always the cheaper starting point. You are mainly paying for time and some compost. Raised beds add the frame, the fill and sometimes delivery, and university extension guidance notes that [the cost can climb quickly with the height and volume of the bed](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/what-are-some-pros-and-cons-growing-vegetables-raised-beds). If you're pricing a wider project, our [DIY garden makeover plan](/notes/diy-garden-makeover-plan) shows where beds fit in the sequence.

**Soil.** This is where raised beds earn their keep. Extension services describe them as a practical answer to [poor soil, and to sites where the native soil is contaminated or unsuitable for vegetables](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/es/catalog/fs-270-raised-bed-gardening). The University of Delaware describes a raised bed as keeping roots [in soil held above waterlogged, potentially contaminated or compacted ground](https://www.udel.edu/udel/en/academics/colleges/canr/cooperative-extension/fact-sheets/raised-bed-gardening/). If you suspect your ground has a history, such as an old building plot or a very old painted structure, a raised bed filled with good soil is a sensible route. Testing the soil first is better still.

If your soil is simply heavy or light rather than contaminated, in-ground beds can work well with patient improvement. It's worth reading our note on [clay soil vs sandy soil and what will grow](/notes/clay-soil-vs-sandy-soil-what-will-grow) before you decide to import anything.

## Do Raised Beds Drain Better?

Yes. Raising the soil lifts roots clear of sitting water, and the same extension guidance notes that raised beds can [dry out and warm up earlier in spring, allowing earlier planting](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/what-are-some-pros-and-cons-growing-vegetables-raised-beds). That's a real advantage on wet ground. But be realistic about scale. If the whole garden is genuinely boggy, a few raised beds treat the symptom in one corner. Our guide to [what to do with a boggy, waterlogged garden](/notes/boggy-waterlogged-garden-what-to-do) covers the bigger picture.

---

## Do Raised Beds Dry Out Faster?

They do, and it's the main price of better drainage. The RHS notes that plants in raised beds [suffer more quickly and more severely from drought than plants in garden soil](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-features/raised-beds), and suggests checking moisture about a finger's depth down and mulching in warm weather. Soaker hoses are a popular fix, as the University of Delaware's guidance on [raised beds](https://www.udel.edu/udel/en/academics/colleges/canr/cooperative-extension/fact-sheets/raised-bed-gardening/) and Oregon State both suggest. Taller beds lose water faster still. If you tend to forget the watering can, in-ground beds with a thick mulch are more forgiving.

**Pests.** Raised beds are not a pest shield. Slugs, snails and others will still find them, though a defined bed does make it easier to net, fleece or cover crops. We wouldn't promise fewer pests either way.

## What Should the Frame Be Made Of?

An in-ground bed has nothing to rot. A raised bed has a frame, and its lifespan depends on the material.

- **Timber.** Economical and easy to work with. The RHS notes that untreated timber deteriorates over time, while pressure-treated softwood sleepers are more durable. If honey fungus is a problem in your garden, the RHS advises avoiding timber and choosing masonry or recycled plastic instead. For edible crops, Oregon State and the University of Delaware both advise avoiding railroad ties and older treated wood. If in doubt, check the supplier's guidance for the product you're buying.
- **Brick, stone or blocks.** Long-lasting and handsome, but the RHS notes that masonry needs skilled construction and, for taller beds, footings.
- **Metal and recycled plastic.** Generally low-maintenance. Recycled plastic is described by the RHS as extremely durable but costly. Check the specification and edge finish of any metal system before buying.

---

## How Big Should a Raised Bed Be?

Whichever route you take, a bed you can't reach is a bed you'll stand on.

- **Width.** The RHS suggests keeping beds under 1.5m (5ft) so you can reach the middle without stepping in. US extension guidance sits at about 4ft (1.2m), and the University of Alaska suggests no more than 48 inches in wet climates, with 36 inches preferable. Narrower still is better if you'll be reaching from a seated position.
- **Depth.** The RHS says 30cm (1ft) is enough for salad leaves, dwarf bulbs and strawberries, but most plants, particularly fruit bushes, shrubs and small trees, need 45cm (18in) or more. Oregon State flags that taller beds over longer runs may need reinforcing.
- **Length and paths.** The RHS suggests 45cm paths for a wheelbarrow and about 1m if a wheelchair needs access. A bed that's easy to reach from both sides is worth far more than one that squeezes in an extra half-metre of growing space.

If you're gardening with limited mobility, raised beds can be built to a comfortable working height, which extension guidance cites as one of their main benefits.

## So Which One Should You Choose?

**Raised beds are likely to suit you if:**

- Your soil is compacted, very stony, contaminated or persistently wet.
- You want to reduce bending, or garden with limited mobility.
- Your space is small and you want a tidy, defined growing area.
- You'd like to grow earlier in spring.

**In-ground beds are likely to suit you if:**

- You have a larger plot and a tighter budget.
- Your site is dry or windy and you'd rather not water often.
- You want deep-rooted crops or shrubs with plenty of room.
- Your soil is already decent, or you're happy to improve it over a few seasons.

**A mixture often works best.** A few raised beds close to the house for salads and herbs, with in-ground beds further out for hungrier or bigger crops, is a common and sensible split. The question that settles it is usually where the beds will sit and how much sun and shade they really get. That's hard to judge from memory.

## By region

The default answer above shifts with your climate. Here is how we'd adjust it.

### United Kingdom

For most of the UK, a wet climate and heavy or compacted soil make raised beds a strong default for vegetables. The RHS says raised beds drain more efficiently than garden soil, which is the benefit on clay and in wet winters, but also the reason they can stress quickly in a dry spell. If your ground is waterlogged, the RHS suggests building in late summer when the soil is drier. On a patio or other hard surface, it advises drainage holes in timber sides or gaps in masonry joints roughly every 45cm so water doesn't pool. Gardeners on free-draining sandy or chalky soils in the drier south and east should lean towards in-ground beds with generous mulch, because raised beds will dry out even faster there.

### Ireland

Irish gardens are often wet, and the GIY-sourced guidance in TheJournal.ie notes raised beds mean less soil compaction and better drainage, with soil that dries out and warms earlier. It also points to housing estates where builders left only a thin layer of topsoil over rubble, which is a textbook case for raised beds filled with good soil. Its suggestions are a width of about 1.2m, a depth of at least 25cm, a mix of roughly 60 per cent soil to 40 per cent compost, and building in autumn so the bed settles over winter. If your Irish garden is a coastal, free-draining sand, the dry-out caution above applies to you too.

### United States

Climate matters more than anywhere else here. In cold northern states, raised beds are a clear win for spring: University of Minnesota Extension notes that [soil in raised beds warms more quickly in the spring](https://extension.umn.edu/planting-and-growing-guides/raised-bed-gardens), while the University of Alaska Fairbanks credits better drainage and greater sun exposure for warming the soil and helping with wet, cold, poorly drained ground. In the hot, dry South and Southwest the balance flips. One gardening source puts the amount of water used as the biggest downside of raised beds, as the elevated soil dries out much more quickly than in-ground soil, and recommends drip irrigation. Minnesota's guidance also says to avoid old pressure-treated wood containing arsenic and to use wood labelled for ground contact.

### Australia

Australia's heat makes low beds the safer choice. Organic Gardener magazine's bed-building advice notes that raised beds take a lot of soil to fill and, without a water reservoir in the base, can dry out quickly. Its author prefers low beds of around 200mm contained with untreated hardwood sleepers, or low mounded beds, over tall structures. The same article accepts that raised beds still work well over a solid base, on contaminated urban soil and for gardeners with mobility issues. If you're in a drier state, think low, mulched and on drip irrigation; in cooler, wetter parts such as Tasmania or the Victorian highlands, the UK and northern US reasoning holds more closely. We haven't found a source that compares raised beds state by state, so treat that as our own reading of the climate rather than a finding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are raised beds better than growing in the ground?

For poor, wet, compacted or contaminated soil, and for anyone who wants less bending, yes. For larger plots, tight budgets and hot, dry climates, in-ground beds are usually the better choice, because they are cheaper and hold moisture longer.

### How deep should a raised bed be?

The RHS suggests 30cm (1ft) for shallow-rooted crops such as salad leaves and strawberries, and 45cm (18in) or more for most other plants, including fruit bushes and small trees.

### How wide should a raised bed be?

Aim for a width you can reach across comfortably without stepping on the soil. The RHS suggests under 1.5m (5ft), and US and Irish guidance suggests about 4ft (1.2m). Go narrower if you will be working from a seated position.

### Do raised beds dry out faster?

Yes. Both the RHS and extension services say raised beds dry out faster than ground soil, so they usually need more regular watering. Mulching and soaker hoses or drip irrigation can help.

### Is treated timber safe for vegetable beds?

Avoid railroad ties and older pressure-treated wood for edible crops, as Oregon State and the University of Delaware advise. Newer treated timber labelled for ground contact is widely used, but if you are unsure, choose untreated hardwood, brick or another material.
