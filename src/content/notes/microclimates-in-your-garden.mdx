## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

You bought the same plant twice. One thrived beside the back wall and flowered for years. The other, planted near the bottom of the lawn, was blackened by the first hard frost and never recovered. Same nursery, same label, same zone. It's easy to blame the plant, or yourself, but more often the explanation is simply that those two spots weren't the same place.

That's what a microclimate is: a small patch where temperature, wind, light and moisture differ from the general conditions around it. Our two notes on hardiness, [the UK planting zone guide](/notes/what-planting-zone-is-the-uk-in) and [the beginner's guide to hardiness zones](/notes/plant-hardiness-zones-ireland-beginners-guide), explain what the zone numbers mean. This note covers what they can't: the variation inside your own boundary.

## Why One Zone Can't Describe a Whole Garden

A hardiness rating, whether it's an RHS H-rating or a USDA zone, is a broad guide built from regional climate data. It can't see your hollow, your south-facing wall or your neighbour's leylandii. University extension services make the same point about the US zone map: it doesn't account for microclimates, and topography, wind patterns, buildings, hills and nearby water can all make a spot warmer or cooler than its official zone suggests. The University of Wisconsin's horticulture team describes a garden beside a lake, sheltered by trees and buildings, successfully growing plants from a zone warmer than the one it's officially in.

Put simply, treat the zone as your starting estimate and your own observations as the correction. If the labels themselves are still confusing, our note on [RHS hardiness ratings versus USDA zones](/notes/rhs-hardiness-vs-usda-zones) and the guide to [what plant label symbols mean](/notes/what-do-plant-label-symbols-mean) are worth a read first.

## The Main Microclimates to Look For

### Frost pockets

Cold air is denser than warm air, so on still, clear nights it drains downhill and collects in low spots. The RHS describes how this creates frost pockets that can shorten the growing season, and notes that a barrier such as a solid hedge or fence across a slope can trap the cold air behind it. Making gaps so the air can drain away can ease the problem. University of Illinois Extension gives the same advice from the orchard side: sites where cold air settles are risky for frost-sensitive blossom, and slightly higher ground helps.

You don't need a dramatic hill. A gentle slope towards the bottom boundary, a sunken patio or a dip behind a wall can be enough.

### Sun traps

A sheltered corner facing the sun, especially one backed by brick or stone, warms quickly and stays warm. The RHS notes that a south-facing wall absorbs heat by day and releases it at night, which can help protect against frost. This is where the borderline-hardy plants tend to cope best. Remember the direction flips in the southern hemisphere: in Australia, it's the north-facing wall that gets the sun and holds the heat.

The flip side is dryness. The RHS points out that walls can create a rain shadow close to their base, so soil there can be very dry. A sun trap is warm, but it may need extra watering while plants establish.

### Wind tunnels

Wind is often the overlooked factor. The RHS describes wind as more damaging to plants in Britain than other forms of stress, and its guidance is that a permeable windbreak shelters a much longer distance than a solid barrier does. Solid fences can create turbulence on the lee side rather than calm. The gap between two buildings or a side passage can also funnel wind, so tender or tall plants may struggle there even when the temperature is mild. Wind also dries foliage in winter, which makes cold feel colder to evergreens.

### Urban warmth

Towns and cities are usually a little warmer than the surrounding countryside at night. As the Royal Meteorological Society's Metlink resource explains, brick, concrete and paving soak up heat in the day and give it back slowly, and the difference is greatest on clear, calm nights and much smaller on cloudy, windy ones. If your garden is in a built-up area, surrounded by paving and walls, you may get a few degrees of extra protection compared with a rural garden in the same zone. That's not a guarantee, but it's a reason you may be able to try slightly more tender plants than a rural neighbour.

### Shade from trees and buildings

Canopy and building shade change more than light. Shaded ground stays cooler in summer and can be drier under thirsty trees, while a tree canopy can also soften the worst frosts beneath it. The RHS notes that tree and hedge shade resembles woodland conditions, which suits shade-lovers but not lawns or late-summer sun lovers. If you're working with a darker plot, our guide to [what will grow in a north-facing garden](/notes/what-will-grow-in-a-north-facing-garden) goes deeper.

### Proximity to water

Larger bodies of water change temperature more slowly than land, which can moderate extremes nearby. For most gardens this is a minor factor, but if you live beside a lake, river or the coast it's worth noting, as is the damp, cold air that can gather in low ground by a stream.

## How to Map Your Garden Over a Season

You don't need instruments. We've found a simple notebook method gets you most of the way, and the RHS suggests a similar approach: observe the surroundings, use a compass for orientation, look for wind-bent trees and check for watercourses in low ground.

1. **Sketch the plot.** A rough bird's-eye outline with the house, boundaries, trees and any slope. Add north with a compass or phone.
2. **Photograph the light.** Take photos from the same spot at morning, midday and late afternoon, and repeat in different seasons. Low winter sun reaches very different places from the high summer sun.
3. **Check after the first frost.** On a clear, cold morning, walk the garden early. Note where frost lingers, where it melted first and where it never formed. Frost-white lawn at the bottom and clear ground by the wall tells you a lot.
4. **Note the wind.** After a windy day, see where leaves pile up, where plants are bent and where the air feels still.
5. **Watch the water.** After heavy rain, record where it pools and where soil stays bone dry, especially under walls and eaves.
6. **Colour in the map.** Mark warm and sheltered, cold and low, windy, and dry zones. A cheap min-max thermometer in two or three spots can confirm what your eyes suggest, if you want numbers.

Doing this across autumn, winter and spring gives a far more honest picture than any single afternoon.

## Using the Map to Place Borderline-Hardy Plants

Once you know your spots, the logic is straightforward.

- **Borderline-hardy plants** (the ones rated just on the edge for your area) go in the warm, sheltered spots, ideally against a wall facing the sun, away from frost pockets and wind.
- **Frost-sensitive early bloomers**, such as magnolias or early-flowering fruit blossom, do better away from the frost pocket, where a late frost won't catch the flowers.
- **Hardy, tough plants** can take the cold hollow and the windy gap. Choose well-proven plants there rather than gambling.
- **Fix before you plant.** A hedge or fence blocking cold air drainage might be improved by opening a gap. A solid windbreak might do better as a more permeable screen.
- **Be honest about risk.** Placement improves your odds, but it doesn't remove them. Mulching, fleece in cold snaps and choosing slightly bigger plants can all help, and it's worth treating any borderline plant as an experiment you're happy to learn from.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a microclimate in a garden?

It's a small area where temperature, wind, light or moisture differ from the surrounding conditions. Frost pockets, sunny walled corners and windy gaps are common examples, even within a small plot.

### How do I find a frost pocket in my garden?

Look for low ground where cold air can settle, or places where a hedge or wall blocks downhill air flow. The simplest check is to walk the garden after a clear, cold night and note where frost lingers longest.

### Does a south-facing wall really protect plants?

It can help. Masonry absorbs heat in the day and releases it overnight, which may soften cold. It isn't a guarantee, and the soil at the base can be very dry. In the southern hemisphere, the equivalent is a north-facing wall.

### Are town gardens warmer than country gardens?

Often slightly, particularly on clear, calm nights, because buildings and paving store and release heat. The effect shrinks on cloudy, windy nights, so it's a bonus rather than something to rely on.

### Can I grow borderline-hardy plants if I have a cold garden?

Sometimes, by choosing the warmest, most sheltered spot, protecting them in winter and accepting some risk. Check what's recommended for your area, and don't plant anything you'd be heartbroken to lose in the coldest spots.
