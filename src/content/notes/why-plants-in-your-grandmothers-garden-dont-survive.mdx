<Callout type="note">
  **Quick answer.** There is rarely one reason. A plant that thrived in a relative's garden and sulks in yours is usually dealing with several things at once: a different site and soil, a changed climate pattern (wetter winters, milder spells, drier summers), newer cultivars, new pests, and a garden that has itself changed. The good news is that most of these are things you can plan around.
</Callout>

Picture the garden you remember. There was a lavender hedge humming with bees, or a fuchsia that came back every spring without anyone fussing over it, or a rose by the back door that seemed to bloom for months. You loved it, so you bought the same plant, gave it a good spot and a careful first summer, and then watched it fade by the second winter.

It is easy to blame yourself. Please don't. Gardens are not recipes, and a plant that thrived in one place is not a promise about another.

One honest note before we start: nobody can look at a single dead plant and say "that was climate change". Weather varies hugely from year to year, and a plant can fail for a dozen ordinary reasons. What we can say is that several things have been shifting at once, and together they explain a good deal of the "it never did this for Nan" experience.

## The Climate Your Plant Grew Up In Has Moved

In the UK, the State of the UK Climate 2024 report (a Met Office-led annual assessment, summarised by the Royal Meteorological Society) says the 2015 to 2024 decade was 1.24°C warmer than the 1961 to 1990 average and about 10% wetter. Winter 2023 to 2024 was the wettest on record, and 2024 had the lowest number of ground frost days on record. Spring leaf-out for trees was also 7 to 11 days earlier than the 1999 to 2023 average in 2024 ([Royal Meteorological Society](https://www.rmets.org/metmatters/state-uk-climate-2024)).

Ireland tells a similar story. Met Éireann's 1991 to 2020 averages put the average annual temperature 0.7°C above the 1961 to 1990 period, with annual rainfall up by around 7%, and more in the west and north ([Government of Ireland, Met Éireann press release](https://www.gov.ie/en/press-release/5fccc-ireland-has-become-warmer-and-wetter-in-the-last-thirty-years-met-eireann-publishes-irelands-new-climate-averages-for-1991-2020)).

In Australia the picture differs by region. CSIRO and the Bureau of Meteorology report that the country has warmed on average by 1.51°C over land since 1910, that April to October rainfall in the south-west has fallen by 16% since 1970, and that heavy rainfall events are becoming more frequent and intense ([CSIRO, State of the Climate 2024](https://www.csiro.au/en/news/All/Articles/2024/November/State-of-the-Climate)).

What does that mean for a border? Fewer hard frosts can be kind to borderline-hardy plants, but they can also coax growth out early, ahead of a late cold snap. Warmer, wetter winters can be harder than a dry freeze for plants that dislike wet roots. And a garden planted fifty years ago was planted for a slightly different climate than the one it now sits in.

## The Zone Maps Have Been Updated Too

If you garden in the US, or you compare yourself to American zones, there is a neat piece of evidence here. In 2023 the USDA released an updated Plant Hardiness Zone Map using 1991 to 2020 temperature data. About half the country shifted to the next warmer half zone, and the other half stayed put, with the shifts representing roughly 0 to 5°F of warming in the areas that changed ([USDA Agricultural Research Service](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDAARS/bulletins/37b2d83)).

An honest caveat: the USDA itself says these updates are not necessarily reflective of global climate change, because the coldest night of the year is so variable, and because the new map uses more sophisticated mapping methods and many more weather stations (13,412, against 7,983 in 2012).

So a changed zone number is part real shift and part better measurement. It is a useful reminder that zone numbers are a snapshot, not a fixed fact. If you want to translate between systems, our [RHS to USDA guide](/notes/rhs-hardiness-vs-usda-zones) explains how the scales line up, and for where the UK sits, see [what planting zone the UK is in](/notes/what-planting-zone-is-the-uk-in). Irish readers will find the same in our [Irish hardiness guide](/notes/plant-hardiness-zones-ireland-beginners-guide).

## Wet Feet Are Often the Real Culprit

When a plant that is "hardy enough" dies over winter, cold gets the blame. Often the cause is water.

The RHS makes the point plainly: choosing plants for soil that is wet in winter and dry in summer is becoming a necessity, and plants that tolerate dry soil or occasional waterlogging avoid being lost to waterlogged conditions ([RHS, How to choose plants for seasonally wet and dry soils](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/for-places/wet-dry-soils)).

Lavender, rosemary, many salvias and other plants from sunny, free-draining places are the classic examples. In a drier decade with lighter soil they can look effortless. Put the same plant in heavier ground through a run of very wet winters and the roots simply sit in cold water. If that sounds like your garden, our guides to [boggy and waterlogged ground](/notes/boggy-waterlogged-garden-what-to-do) and [clay versus sandy soil](/notes/clay-soil-vs-sandy-soil-what-will-grow) are good places to start, and the opposite problem is covered in [drought-tolerant planting](/notes/drought-tolerant-water-wise-planting).

## Your Garden Is Not Her Garden

Even with the same weather overhead, two gardens a few streets apart can behave like different climates. A few of the usual suspects:

- **Soil.** Clay, sand, chalk, acidic or alkaline: a plant that loved her loam may resent your clay.
- **Shelter and aspect.** A south-facing wall, a sheltered courtyard or the lee of a big hedge can keep a plant several degrees warmer than an open lawn. The RHS notes that H3 plants may be hardy elsewhere with wall shelter or a good microclimate, and that H5 plants may not withstand open or exposed sites ([RHS, Hardiness ratings](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/trials-awards/award-of-garden-merit/rhs-hardiness-rating)).
- **Frost pockets.** Cold air flows downhill and pools. A low corner can be much colder than the top of the same garden.
- **Exposure.** Wind, coastal salt and open ground are tougher than the regional average suggests.

Our guides to [microclimates](/notes/microclimates-in-your-garden) and [garden orientation](/notes/right-plant-right-place-garden-orientation) show how to read your own plot. The RHS calls its ratings a general guide only, so treat the label as the start of the conversation.

## Other Things That Quietly Change

Climate is only part of it. A few other shifts are easy to miss.

**The plant itself.** The "same" plant is not always the same. Nurseries select and breed new cultivars for flower colour, compactness and speed of growth, and a modern variety is not guaranteed to match the toughness of the old one your relative grew. 

**Pests and diseases.** Some problems simply were not here a generation ago. The RHS reports that the box tree moth first appeared in south-east England in 2007, reached private gardens in 2011 and is now found across the UK and Ireland. In early 2024, reports were almost five times those of the previous year, which RHS scientists linked to a mild winter followed by a warm spring ([RHS, Box tree moth](https://www.rhs.org.uk/science/articles/2024-to-be-bumper-year-for-box-moth)). If your relative's neat box hedge is a happy memory and yours is a struggle, that is a very real reason.

**The garden has grown up.** A new fence, a patio, a neighbour's extension or a tree that has doubled in size can change light, wind, rainfall reaching the soil and root competition. A border that was sunny when you moved in may be shady now.

**Knowing the plot.** A relative may have known their garden for forty years: where the frost sat, which corner was dry, which plants to lift in a bad winter. That quiet knowledge is a big part of why gardens look easy.

## What You Can Do About It

There is a hopeful side to all of this.

1. **Start from your garden as it is now.** Walk it at different times of day, note where frost lingers and where the wall holds warmth, and check how the soil drains after rain.
2. **Match plants to conditions, not to memories.** Keep the spirit of the plant you loved, such as the scent, the colour or the wildlife, and look for something that suits your site. A tough alternative often gives you the same feeling.
3. **Plant borderline plants where they have help.** A sheltered wall, good drainage and a little winter mulch can make a real difference.
4. **Spread the risk.** Mix several reliable plants rather than betting a whole border on one fussy favourite. If you're weighing native against other plants, see our note on [native versus non-native planting](/notes/native-vs-non-native-planting).
5. **Time things sensibly.** Early springs and odd frosts mean it pays to know your [last frost dates](/notes/last-frost-dates-and-planting-timing).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do plants that grew well for my parents or grandparents struggle in my garden?

Usually several things at once: different soil and shelter, wetter winters and warmer, more changeable seasons than past decades, different cultivars, new pests and a garden that has itself changed. It is rarely one cause, and it is rarely your fault.

### Is climate change the reason my plants are dying?

It is very hard to say for any single plant, and we would not claim it. Weather varies a lot year to year, and soil, drainage, exposure, pests and planting care all matter. What published records do show is warmer, and in many places wetter, conditions than in the mid-twentieth century, which may make some plants harder or easier to grow.

### Did the USDA move my zone, and does it matter?

The 2023 USDA map moved about half the country to a warmer half zone, but the USDA says some of that reflects better data and mapping, not only warming. Treat your zone as a rough guide and let your own garden's conditions have the final word.

### Why do "hardy" plants die in mild winters?

Often it is wet soil, not cold. Plants from dry, sunny climates dislike sitting in cold, waterlogged ground, and hardiness ratings are a general guide only. Better drainage and a more sheltered position can help.

### Can I still grow my grandmother's favourite plants?

Often, yes. Try them in the most suitable spot you have, improve drainage if needed, and consider a close relative of the plant that suits your conditions better. A plan based on your own garden can show you where each plant has the best chance.
