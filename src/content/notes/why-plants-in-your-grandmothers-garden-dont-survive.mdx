  **Quick answer.** Usually not one cause but several: a different site and soil, wetter and more changeable winters than past decades, newer cultivars, pests that were not here a generation ago, and a garden that has itself changed. Nobody can pin a single lost plant on climate, but most of these causes are things you can plan around by choosing plants for the garden you have now.

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture the garden you remember. There was a lavender hedge humming with bees, or a fuchsia that came back every spring without anyone fussing over it, or a rose by the back door that seemed to bloom for months. You loved it, so you bought the same plant, gave it a good spot and a careful first summer, and then watched it fade by the second winter.

It is easy to blame yourself. Please don't. Gardens are not recipes, and a plant that thrived in one place is not a promise about another.

One honest note before we start: nobody can look at a single dead plant and say "that was climate change". Weather swings hugely from year to year, and a plant can fail for a dozen ordinary reasons. What we can say is that several things have shifted at once, and together they explain a good deal of the "it never did this for Nan" experience.

---

## Has the Climate Your Plant Grew Up In Moved?

Measured records say it has, a little. The State of the UK Climate 2024 report found the 2015 to 2024 decade was 1.24°C warmer and 10% wetter than the 1961 to 1990 average. Winter 2023 to 2024 was the wettest season on record, and 2024 recorded the lowest number of ground frost days on record. Tree first-leaf dates in 2024 were 7 to 11 days earlier than the 1999 to 2023 average ([Royal Meteorological Society](https://www.rmets.org/metmatters/state-uk-climate-2024)).

What does that mean for a border? Fewer hard frosts can be kind to borderline-hardy plants, but they can also coax growth out early, ahead of a late cold snap. Warmer, wetter winters can be harder than a dry freeze for plants that dislike wet roots. And a garden planted fifty years ago was planted for a slightly different climate than the one it now sits in. Our look at how [British gardens are quietly changing](/notes/your-own-british-vineyard) follows that thread through to grapes.

## Do Zone Maps Show the Change?

Partly. In 2023 the USDA released an updated Plant Hardiness Zone Map using 1991 to 2020 temperature data. About half the country shifted to the next warmer half zone, and the other half stayed put, representing roughly 0 to 5°F of warming in the areas that moved ([USDA Agricultural Research Service](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDAARS/bulletins/37b2d83)).

The caveat matters. The map's developers say the changes are not necessarily reflective of global climate change, because the coldest night of the year is so variable. The new map also uses more sophisticated methods and far more weather stations (13,412, against 7,983 for the 2012 version).

So a changed zone number is part real shift and part better measurement. It is a reminder that zone numbers are a snapshot, not a fixed fact. To translate between systems, see our [RHS to USDA guide](/notes/rhs-hardiness-vs-usda-zones), or [what planting zone the UK is in](/notes/what-planting-zone-is-the-uk-in).

## Why Do "Hardy" Plants Die When Winters Turn Wet?

When a plant that is "hardy enough" dies over winter, cold gets the blame. Often the cause is water.

The RHS makes the point plainly: choosing plants for soil that is wet in winter and dry in summer is becoming a necessity, and plants that tolerate dry soil or occasional waterlogging avoid being lost to waterlogged conditions ([RHS, How to choose plants for seasonally wet and dry soils](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/for-places/wet-dry-soils)).

Lavender, rosemary, many salvias and other plants from sunny, free-draining places are the classic examples. In a drier decade on lighter soil they can look effortless. Put the same plant in heavier ground through a run of very wet winters and the roots simply sit in cold water. If that sounds like your garden, start with our guides to [boggy and waterlogged ground](/notes/boggy-waterlogged-garden-what-to-do) and [clay versus sandy soil](/notes/clay-soil-vs-sandy-soil-what-will-grow).

## Is Your Garden Different From Hers?

Even with the same weather overhead, two gardens a few streets apart can behave like different climates.

- **Soil.** Clay, sand, chalk, acidic or alkaline: a plant that loved her loam may resent your clay.
- **Shelter and aspect.** A south-facing wall, a sheltered courtyard or the lee of a big hedge can keep a plant several degrees warmer than an open lawn. The RHS notes that H3 plants may be hardy elsewhere with wall shelter or a good microclimate, and that H5 plants may not withstand open or exposed sites ([RHS, Hardiness ratings](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/trials-awards/award-of-garden-merit/rhs-hardiness-rating)).
- **Frost pockets.** Cold air flows downhill and pools, so a low corner can be much colder than the top of the same garden.
- **Exposure.** Wind, coastal salt and open ground are tougher than the regional average suggests.

Our guides to [microclimates](/notes/microclimates-in-your-garden) and [garden orientation](/notes/right-plant-right-place-garden-orientation) show how to read your own plot. The RHS calls its ratings a general guide only, so treat the label as the start of the conversation.

## What Else Has Quietly Changed?

**The plant itself.** The "same" plant is not always the same. Nurseries select and breed new cultivars for flower colour, compactness and speed of growth, and a modern variety is not guaranteed to match the toughness of the old one your relative grew.

**Pests and diseases.** Some problems simply were not here a generation ago. The RHS reports that the box tree moth first appeared in south-east England in 2007, reached private gardens in 2011 and is now found across the UK and Ireland. In early 2024, reports were almost five times those of the previous year, which RHS scientists linked to a mild winter followed by a warm spring ([RHS, Box tree moth](https://www.rhs.org.uk/science/articles/2024-to-be-bumper-year-for-box-moth)). If your relative's neat box hedge is a happy memory and yours is a struggle, that is a very real reason.

**The garden has grown up.** A new fence, a patio, a neighbour's extension or a tree that has doubled in size can change light, wind, rainfall reaching the soil and root competition. A border that was sunny when you moved in may be shady now.

**Knowing the plot.** A relative may have known their garden for forty years: where the frost sat, which corner was dry, which plants to lift in a bad winter. That quiet knowledge is a big part of why gardens look easy.

## What Can You Do About It?

There is a hopeful side to all of this.

1. **Start from your garden as it is now.** Walk it at different times of day, note where frost lingers and where the wall holds warmth, and check how the soil drains after rain.
2. **Match plants to conditions, not to memories.** Keep the spirit of the plant you loved, such as the scent, the colour or the wildlife, and look for something that suits your site.
3. **Plant borderline plants where they have help.** A sheltered wall, good drainage and a little winter mulch can make a real difference.
4. **Spread the risk.** Mix several reliable plants rather than betting a whole border on one fussy favourite. If you are weighing native against other plants, see [native versus non-native planting](/notes/native-vs-non-native-planting).
5. **Time things sensibly.** Early springs and odd frosts mean it pays to know your [last frost dates](/notes/last-frost-dates-and-planting-timing).

## By region

What changed, and what to do about it, differs by where you garden. Here is what the published sources support.

### UK

The decade-level picture is warmer and wetter than the 1961 to 1990 average, with record-low ground frost days in 2024 (figures above). The Met Office's regional climate summaries describe 11 UK regions and note that places in the east and south tend to be drier, warmer, sunnier and less windy than those further west and north ([Met Office, UK regional climates](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/maps-and-data/regional-climates/index)). In practice, wet-winter losses are most likely for drainage-hungry plants like lavender in western and northern gardens on heavy soil, while eastern gardens are more likely to need drought-tolerant choices.

### Ireland

Met Éireann's 1991 to 2020 averages put the average annual temperature at 9.8°C, 0.7°C above the 1961 to 1990 period, with spring showing the largest seasonal rise (0.8°C). Annual rainfall is up about 7% overall: roughly 3 to 6% in the east against roughly 6 to 12% across areas of the west and north. Winter and autumn are the wettest seasons ([Government of Ireland, Met Éireann](https://www.gov.ie/en/press-release/5fccc-ireland-has-become-warmer-and-wetter-in-the-last-thirty-years-met-eireann-publishes-irelands-new-climate-averages-for-1991-2020)). For gardeners in the west and north, that points to drainage and shelter first. Our [Irish hardiness guide](/notes/plant-hardiness-zones-ireland-beginners-guide) explains the zones.

### United States

The 2023 USDA map moved about half the country up a half zone, but the USDA cautions that this is not a reliable indicator of global climate change. Check your new zone, then let your own frost pockets and soil have the final word.

### Australia

CSIRO and the Bureau of Meteorology report that Australia has warmed on average by 1.51°C over land since 1910. April to October rainfall has decreased by 16% in the south-west since 1970 and by 9% in the south-east since 1994, while northern wet-season rainfall has risen by about 20% since 1994. Heavy rainfall events are increasing in frequency and intensity ([CSIRO, State of the Climate 2024](https://www.csiro.au/en/news/All/Articles/2024/November/State-of-the-Climate)). So a plant your grandmother grew in Perth or Melbourne may now face a drier cool season, while a Queensland gardener may see a wetter one: match plants to your own state's trend, not a national average.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do plants that grew well for my parents or grandparents struggle in my garden?

Usually several things at once: different soil and shelter, wetter and more changeable seasons than past decades, different cultivars, new pests and a garden that has itself changed. It is rarely one cause, and it is rarely your fault.

### Is climate change the reason my plants are dying?

For any single plant, no one can say, and we would not claim it. Weather varies a lot year to year, and soil, drainage, exposure, pests and planting care all matter. Published records do show warmer conditions, and in many places wetter ones, than in the mid-twentieth century.

### Did the USDA move my zone, and does it matter?

The 2023 USDA map moved about half the country to a warmer half zone, but part of that reflects better data and mapping rather than warming alone. Treat your zone as a rough guide, not a verdict.

### Why do "hardy" plants die in mild winters?

Often it is wet soil, not cold. Plants from dry, sunny climates dislike sitting in cold, waterlogged ground. Better drainage and a more sheltered position usually help.

### Can I still grow my grandmother's favourite plants?

Often, yes. Try them in the most suitable spot you have, improve drainage if needed, and consider a close relative that suits your conditions better.
