A sloping garden can feel like a problem to be solved, but it is often the most characterful kind of plot. Here is how to assess your slope, choose between the options in order of effort, and pick plants that hold a bank together while looking good.

---

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture it: the house sits at the top, the garden falls away towards the back fence, and every time it rains a little more soil ends up on the path. The lawn is awkward to mow, the patio is the only flat spot, and nobody quite knows where to put a table.

The instinct is often to jump to the big, dramatic fix: a stack of retaining walls, a digger, a skip. Sometimes that's right, but often it isn't, or not yet. The better order is to understand the slope, then choose the lightest intervention that works. That saves money and avoids taking on structural work you didn't need.

---

## How Steep Is Your Slope, Really?

Slopes feel steeper than they are, so measure. You don't need surveying kit.

1. Push a straight stake in at the top of the slope and another at the bottom, or use a long straight plank.
2. Stretch a string between them and use a spirit level to get it horizontal.
3. Measure the horizontal distance (the "run") and the vertical drop from the string to the ground at the lower stake (the "rise").
4. Divide rise by run and multiply by 100 to get a percentage. A 1 metre drop over 10 metres is 10%.

As a rough guide, University of Georgia Extension describes slopes under about 5% as gentle, 8 to 16% as moderately steep and 20% or more as steep, with around 30% too steep to mow and 50% unlikely to support plant growth easily ([University of Georgia Extension](https://site.extension.uga.edu/forsyth/meeting-the-landscape-challenges-of-slopes-and-hillsides)). Mississippi State's extension service also calls land that rises more than 20% steep, roughly two feet of rise for every ten feet of run ([Mississippi State University Extension](https://extension.msstate.edu/lawn-and-garden/landscape-architecture/landscape-design-and-management/landscape-and-garden-design/gardening-steep-slopes)).

Also note where water goes after rain, which way the slope faces (a sunny, free-draining bank behaves very differently from a shady, damp one) and how close it is to the house or boundary. Those details matter as much as the gradient.

## What Are Your Options, From Lightest to Heaviest?

### 1. Planting alone (lowest effort and cost)

For gentle to moderate slopes, dense planting is often enough. Roots bind the soil and foliage slows rain before it hits the ground. The RHS suggests strong-growing ground-cover plants and climbers for steep banks, planted through a mulch, with coarse coir matting pegged down on the steepest sections, and says good cover should be achieved after two growing seasons ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/for-places/banks-and-slopes)). Be patient, because the bank is most vulnerable in the first year.

### 2. Gentle regrading

Softening an awkward bump or hollow, or making a small level area for seating, can transform a slope without building anything. Keep it modest, keep soil away from house walls and fences, and make sure the new levels don't send water towards a building. Larger earthworks are a different matter (see below).

### 3. Steps and paths

Steps make a slope usable. A path that zig-zags across the gradient is gentler to walk and slows runoff compared with one running straight downhill. Keep treads generous and even, add a handrail where the drop is significant, and think about lighting and wet-weather grip. Modest steps suit a competent DIYer, and our note on [hard versus soft landscaping](/notes/hard-landscaping-vs-soft-landscaping) will help you plan the materials.

### 4. Terraces and retaining walls

Terracing breaks one long slope into shorter, flatter steps, which slows water and gives you usable beds or seating areas. Extension guidance describes terraces as a way of reducing erosion by creating shorter, more even steps ([Mississippi State University Extension](https://extension.msstate.edu/lawn-and-garden/landscape-architecture/landscape-design-and-management/landscape-and-garden-design/gardening-steep-slopes)).

Here is the honest bit: a wall holding back soil is under constant pressure, especially when the ground is wet. Extension advice for homeowners is to keep DIY walls to a foot or two in height and bring in a professional for anything bigger, with proper drainage behind the wall ([University of Georgia CAES](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/news/tackle-erosion-with-grasses-landscape-plants-and-terraces/)). A series of low walls is usually safer and kinder to the budget than one tall one.

### 5. Decks on slopes

A deck can give you a level platform without moving much soil, which is why it's popular on steeper plots. Once it sits well above the ground, though, it can become a structure with its own rules about height, guarding and foundations, so check before you design it. Our [guide to hard and soft landscaping](/notes/hard-landscaping-vs-soft-landscaping) compares surfaces in more detail.

## When Should You Get Professional Advice?

Get it before building any retaining wall over roughly a metre, and before any major regrading. These are structural jobs: a wall near a boundary affects your neighbour, and a failure can be costly and dangerous. Ask an engineer or experienced contractor to confirm the design and drainage. If you're weighing up who should do what, our notes on [DIY or hiring a landscaper](/notes/diy-or-hire-a-landscaper) and [what to ask before you hire](/notes/before-you-hire-a-landscaper) will help. The rules themselves vary by country and council, so see the regional section below.

## How Do You Manage Drainage, Erosion and Safety?

Water is what moves slopes. A few principles help:

- **Slow it down.** Plant densely, mulch bare soil and use terraces or small check dams to break up long runs.
- **Respect natural channels.** Water follows the easiest route, so it's often better to keep or create a deliberate drainage channel than to fight it, with stones or check dams to stop gullies forming ([Mississippi State University Extension](https://extension.msstate.edu/lawn-and-garden/landscape-architecture/landscape-design-and-management/landscape-and-garden-design/gardening-steep-slopes)).
- **Protect the house.** Direct surface water away from walls, not towards them. If the bottom of your slope stays wet, our note on a [boggy, waterlogged garden](/notes/boggy-waterlogged-garden-what-to-do) covers what to do next.
- **Think about people.** Wet leaves, loose gravel and steep lawns are slip hazards. Plan sure-footed paths, handrails and safe places to stand when you work on the bank.

## Which Plants Hold a Slope Together?

The aim is layers: ground cover to protect the surface, perennials and grasses to knit the topsoil, and shrubs for deeper structure. For a sunny, free-draining bank, the RHS suggests a combination including *Ceanothus* 'Dark Star', *Cistus* x *purpureus*, *Genista* 'Porlock', thyme, crocosmia and *Agastache* 'Blue Fortune', noting that their spreading habit and root systems help protect and bind the soil ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-design/sustainable-planting-combinations/problem-solving/slope-stabilisation-sun)). For shadier banks, extension sources point to ivies, ferns and other shade-tolerant ground covers ([Mississippi State University Extension](https://extension.msstate.edu/lawn-and-garden/landscape-architecture/landscape-design-and-management/landscape-and-garden-design/gardening-steep-slopes)).

Some practical tips from the same sources:

- Choose small plants and plant them in staggered groups; they settle with less soil disturbance than seed ([University of Georgia Extension](https://site.extension.uga.edu/forsyth/meeting-the-landscape-challenges-of-slopes-and-hillsides)).
- Make a small level "shelf" around each plant so water soaks in rather than running off.
- Mulch after planting, using material that won't wash away easily on steeper ground.
- Space plants according to the plant label, tighter on poor soil, and water through the first year.

Check that a plant suits your hardiness zone and local conditions before buying, since one that thrives in one country's climate may struggle in another's.

## What Should You Do About a Steep Lawn?

Replace it, or stop trying to mow it like a flat one. Sources suggest slopes around 30% are too steep to mow comfortably ([University of Georgia Extension](https://site.extension.uga.edu/forsyth/meeting-the-landscape-challenges-of-slopes-and-hillsides)). Options include swapping the steepest part for ground cover or low shrubs, letting it become meadow-style long grass cut once or twice a year, or using a strimmer. Never mow wet grass on a bank.

Maintenance on a planted slope is lighter once plants knit together: weeding in the early years, topping up mulch, and checking after heavy rain for signs of gullies or slumping.

## By region

Thresholds for permission and engineering sign-off differ widely, and the figures below are examples from the authorities we checked, not national rules. Always check with your own local authority before building anything substantial.

### United Kingdom

Building control and planning are separate questions. Swansea Council says a building regulations application, with construction details and calculations, is needed for a retaining wall over 1.5 metres in height, new or extended, and that planning permission may also be required, so you should check its planning pages as well ([Swansea Council](https://www.swansea.gov.uk/article/6623/Retaining-walls)). Other councils may set different thresholds, so ask your own planning and building control teams, particularly in England, Scotland and Northern Ireland where we haven't verified local rules. For planting, the RHS guidance above is written for UK conditions.

### Ireland

Garden walls and fences are often exempted development, meaning no planning permission is needed. The Society of Chartered Surveyors Ireland summarises this as up to 2 metres for rear walls and fences and 1.2 metres in front gardens, and says that retaining walls aren't specifically addressed in the excerpt it discusses ([SCSI](https://scsi.ie/confused-about-planning-regulations-for-height-of-garden-wall/)). Exemptions don't apply in places such as an Architectural Conservation Area or for a protected structure ([Galway County Council](https://galway.ie/en/services/planning/process/exempt)). If you're unsure, you can ask your local authority for a Section 5 declaration on whether works are exempted development; Galway describes a written application with site maps and a fee, decided within four weeks ([Galway County Council](https://galway.ie/en/services/planning/process/exempt)). Fees are quoted by the councils and can change, so confirm the current amount. A tall retaining wall is still a structural matter, so involve an engineer regardless of planning status.

### United States

Rules are set locally by city, county and state. Portland, Oregon requires a permit for a retaining wall over 4 feet measured from the bottom of the footing, or where the wall supports a surcharge (extra load such as a driveway or building above), and requires stamped engineer's drawings for walls over 4 feet ([City of Portland](https://www.portland.gov/bds/retaining-wall-permits)). Carroll County, Maryland also requires a building permit above 4 feet, and says engineered plans may be required ([Carroll County](https://carrollcountymd.gov/government/directory/public-works/permits-inspections/residential-projects/building-a-retaining-wall)). Cooperative extension guidance from Georgia and Mississippi, cited above, is the best starting point for slope planting in the South; look for your own state's extension service elsewhere.

### Australia

Brisbane City Council generally doesn't require planning approval for a stepped or terraced landscaping wall under one metre in height that is set back from boundaries. It doesn't require building approval if the total height is no more than one metre above the natural ground surface, there is no surcharge loading, and the wall is at least 1.5 metres from a building or another retaining wall. All conditions must be met together, otherwise certification is needed ([Brisbane City Council](https://www.brisbane.qld.gov.au/planning-building/do-i-need-approval/residential-projects/yards-pools-fences-landscaping/build-retaining-wall)). Other states and councils have their own rules, so check locally.

### What about rainfall and erosion by region?

We haven't sourced region-by-region rainfall figures for this note, so rather than guess, treat any area with heavy or intense rain as higher risk and lean towards denser planting, mulch and proper drainage behind walls, as the extension guidance above suggests.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I measure the slope in my garden?

Run a level string between two stakes at the top and bottom, measure the vertical drop and the horizontal distance, then divide rise by run and multiply by 100 for a percentage.

### What is the cheapest way to landscape a sloped garden?

Planting. On gentle to moderate slopes, ground cover, perennials and shrubs with mulch protect the soil while they establish, and cost far less than walls or groundwork.

### Do I need permission to build a retaining wall?

Often not for a low wall, but usually yes once it gets tall. Examples include over 1.5 metres in Swansea, over 4 feet in Portland and Carroll County, and over one metre in Brisbane. Check with your local authority first.

### What plants stop soil erosion on a slope?

Dense, spreading plants with fibrous roots work well, such as thyme, cistus, ceanothus, crocosmia and ivies, chosen for your sun, shade and climate.

### Can I mow a steep lawn safely?

Not past about 30%, according to extension guidance. Replace the steepest areas with ground cover or long grass if mowing feels unsafe.
