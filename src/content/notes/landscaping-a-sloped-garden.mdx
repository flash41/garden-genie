A sloping garden can feel like a problem to be solved, but it's often the most characterful kind of plot. This guide walks through how to assess your slope, the main options in order of effort, and the plants that will hold a bank together while looking good.

---

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture it: the house sits at the top, the garden falls away towards the back fence, and every time it rains, a little more soil ends up on the path. The lawn is awkward to mow, the patio is the only flat spot, and nobody quite knows where to put a table.

It's a very common situation, and the instinct is often to jump straight to the big, dramatic fix: a stack of retaining walls, a digger, a skip. Sometimes that's right. Often it isn't, or at least not yet. The better order is to understand the slope first, then choose the lightest intervention that works. That saves money, and it avoids taking on structural work you didn't need.

---

## First, Work Out How Steep Your Slope Really Is

Slopes feel steeper than they are, so measure. You don't need surveying kit.

1. Push a straight stake in at the top of the slope and another at the bottom, or use a long straight plank.
2. Stretch a string between them and use a spirit level to get it horizontal.
3. Measure the horizontal distance (the "run") and the vertical drop from the string to the ground at the lower stake (the "rise").
4. Divide rise by run and multiply by 100 to get a percentage. A 1 metre drop over 10 metres is 10%.

As a rough guide, university extension guidance in the US describes slopes under about 5% as gentle, 8 to 16% as moderately steep and 20% or more as steep, with slopes of around 30% too steep to mow and 50% unlikely to support plant growth easily ([University of Georgia Extension](https://site.extension.uga.edu/forsyth/meeting-the-landscape-challenges-of-slopes-and-hillsides)). Mississippi State's extension service uses a similar threshold, calling land that rises more than 20% steep, roughly two feet of rise for every ten feet of run ([Mississippi State University Extension](https://extension.msstate.edu/lawn-and-garden/landscape-architecture/landscape-design-and-management/landscape-and-garden-design/gardening-steep-slopes)).

Also note where water goes after rain, which way the slope faces (a sunny, free-draining bank behaves very differently from a shady, damp one) and how close the slope is to the house or boundary. Those details matter as much as the gradient.

## Your Options, From Lightest to Heaviest

### 1. Planting alone (lowest effort and cost)

For gentle to moderate slopes, dense planting is often enough. Roots bind the soil and foliage slows the rain before it hits the ground. The RHS suggests strong-growing ground-cover plants and climbers for steep banks, planted through a mulch, with coarse coir matting pegged down on the steepest sections, and says good cover should be achieved after two growing seasons ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/for-places/banks-and-slopes)). Be patient, because the bank is most vulnerable in the first year.

### 2. Gentle regrading

Softening an awkward bump or hollow, or making a small level area for seating, can transform a slope without building anything. Keep it modest, keep soil away from house walls and fences, and make sure the new levels don't send water towards a building. Larger earthworks are a different matter (see below).

### 3. Steps and paths

Steps make a slope usable. A path that zig-zags across the gradient is gentler to walk and slows runoff compared with one running straight downhill. Keep treads generous and even, add a handrail where the drop is significant, and think about lighting and wet-weather grip. This is a good job for competent DIYers if the steps are modest, but it's worth reading our note on [hard versus soft landscaping](/notes/hard-landscaping-vs-soft-landscaping) to plan the materials.

### 4. Terraces and retaining walls

Terracing breaks one long slope into shorter, flatter steps, which slows water and gives you usable beds or seating areas. Extension guidance describes terraces as a way of reducing erosion by creating shorter, more even steps ([Mississippi State University Extension](https://extension.msstate.edu/lawn-and-garden/landscape-architecture/landscape-design-and-management/landscape-and-garden-design/gardening-steep-slopes)).

Here is the honest bit: a wall holding back soil is under constant pressure, especially when the ground is wet. Extension advice for homeowners is to keep DIY walls to a foot or two in height and to bring in a professional for anything bigger, with proper drainage behind the wall ([University of Georgia CAES](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/news/tackle-erosion-with-grasses-landscape-plants-and-terraces/)). A series of low walls is usually safer and kinder to the budget than one tall one.

### 5. Decks on slopes

A deck can give you a level platform without moving much soil, which is why it's popular on steeper plots. But once a deck sits well above the ground it can become a structure with its own rules about height, guarding and foundations. We compare surfaces in more detail in our [guide to hard and soft landscaping](/notes/hard-landscaping-vs-soft-landscaping).

## When It Becomes Structural: Get Advice First

Retaining walls over roughly a metre and any major regrading are structural work. Rules differ by country, council and even street, so treat the figures below only as examples of how much they vary. One Welsh council says a wall retaining land over 1.5 metres needs an application with construction details and calculations ([Swansea Council](https://www.swansea.gov.uk/article/6623/Retaining-walls)), while Brisbane City Council in Australia generally expects approval once total wall height goes beyond one metre, or where other conditions apply ([Brisbane City Council](https://www.brisbane.qld.gov.au/planning-building/do-i-need-approval/residential-projects/yards-pools-fences-landscaping/build-retaining-wall)).

So whether you're in the UK, Ireland, the US or Australia, check with your local authority's planning and building control teams before building anything substantial, and ask an engineer or experienced contractor to confirm the design. Remember too that a wall near a boundary affects your neighbour. ## Drainage, Erosion and Safety

Water is what moves slopes. A few principles help:

- **Slow it down.** Plant densely, mulch bare soil and use terraces or small check dams to break up long runs.
- **Respect natural channels.** Extension advice is that water follows the easiest route, so it's often better to keep or create a deliberate drainage channel than to fight it, with stones or check dams to stop gullies forming ([Mississippi State University Extension](https://extension.msstate.edu/lawn-and-garden/landscape-architecture/landscape-design-and-management/landscape-and-garden-design/gardening-steep-slopes)).
- **Protect the house.** Make sure surface water is directed away from walls, not towards them. If the bottom of your slope stays wet, our note on a [boggy, waterlogged garden](/notes/boggy-waterlogged-garden-what-to-do) covers what to do next.
- **Think about people.** Wet leaves, loose gravel and steep lawns are slip hazards. Plan sure-footed paths, handrails and safe places to stand when you work on the bank.

## Plants That Hold a Slope Together

The aim is layers: ground cover to protect the surface, perennials and grasses to knit the topsoil, and shrubs for deeper structure. For a sunny, free-draining bank, the RHS suggests a combination including *Ceanothus* 'Dark Star', *Cistus* x *purpureus*, *Genista* 'Porlock', thyme, crocosmia and *Agastache* 'Blue Fortune', noting that the spreading habit and root systems of these plants help protect and bind the soil ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-design/sustainable-planting-combinations/problem-solving/slope-stabilisation-sun)). For shadier banks, extension sources point to ivies, ferns and other shade-tolerant ground covers ([Mississippi State University Extension](https://extension.msstate.edu/lawn-and-garden/landscape-architecture/landscape-design-and-management/landscape-and-garden-design/gardening-steep-slopes)).

Some practical tips from the same sources:

- Choose small plants and plant them in staggered groups; they settle with less soil disturbance than seed ([University of Georgia Extension](https://site.extension.uga.edu/forsyth/meeting-the-landscape-challenges-of-slopes-and-hillsides)).
- Make a small level "shelf" around each plant so water soaks in rather than running off.
- Mulch after planting, using material that won't wash away easily on steeper ground.
- Space plants according to the plant label, tighter on poor soil, and water through the first year.

Always check that a plant suits your hardiness zone and local conditions before buying, since a plant that thrives in one country's climate may struggle in another's.

## Maintenance: Rethink the Steep Lawn

A steep lawn is hard work and can be risky to mow. Sources suggest that slopes around 30% are too steep to mow comfortably ([University of Georgia Extension](https://site.extension.uga.edu/forsyth/meeting-the-landscape-challenges-of-slopes-and-hillsides)). If mowing makes you nervous, don't force it. Options include replacing the steepest part with ground cover or low shrubs, letting it become meadow-style long grass cut once or twice a year, or using a strimmer. Mow across a gentle slope rather than up and down only if your mower's guidance allows, and never mow wet grass on a bank.

Maintenance on a planted slope is lighter once plants knit together: weeding in the early years, topping up mulch, and checking after heavy rain for any signs of gullies or slumping.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I measure the slope in my garden?

Run a level string between two stakes at the top and bottom, then measure the vertical drop and the horizontal distance. Divide rise by run and multiply by 100 for a percentage.

### What is the cheapest way to landscape a sloped garden?

Planting alone is usually the lowest-cost route on gentle to moderate slopes, using ground cover, perennials and shrubs with mulch to protect the soil while they establish.

### Do I need permission to build a retaining wall?

It depends on the wall's height, position and where you live. Some authorities start asking for approval at around a metre, others at higher figures, so check with your local council before building.

### What plants stop soil erosion on a slope?

Dense, spreading plants with fibrous roots work well, such as thyme, cistus, ceanothus, crocosmia and ivies, chosen for your sun, shade and climate.

### Can I mow a steep lawn safely?

Very steep lawns can be dangerous. Consider replacing the steepest areas with ground cover or long grass if mowing feels unsafe.
