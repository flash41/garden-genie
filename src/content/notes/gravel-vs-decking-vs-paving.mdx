<Callout type="note">
  **Quick answer.** For most family gardens, paving for the main sitting area and gravel for paths and secondary spaces gives the best balance of durability, drainage and upkeep. Choose decking instead where the ground slopes or you need a raised level at the back door, and only if the spot is reasonably sunny and well ventilated underneath. Wet, shady or very exposed gardens, and places with local rules, shift the answer, so check the regional notes below.
</Callout>

Most people choose a garden surface the way they choose a sofa: by how it looks in a photo. That's a fine place to start, but it's the wrong place to finish. The surface decides how the garden handles rain, how it feels in January, and how many Saturdays you'll spend scrubbing it.

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture a shady patch at the back of the house. You love the look of a warm timber deck, so you build one. Two winters later it's green and slick by the back door, and you're wondering whether you chose wrong. Or you pick pale paving, and discover that the corner where water pools now has a permanent puddle. Neither is a design failure exactly. Each surface simply met a condition it wasn't suited to.

Match the surface to the conditions and any of the three can work beautifully. If you're still weighing the bigger picture, our note on [hard landscaping vs soft landscaping](/notes/hard-landscaping-vs-soft-landscaping) sets out how surfaces fit with planting.

## Is Paving the Safest Choice for a Main Seating Area?

For most gardens, yes. Paving (pavers, in the US and Australia) covers everything from natural stone to porcelain and concrete block. It's the longest-lived of the three and gives the flattest, most stable surface for tables and chairs.

- **Base is everything.** Gardening Australia's presenter puts it plainly: pavers are only as good as the base. Their worked example uses a compacted road base layer, a sand bed, then the pavers, compacting at each stage, with a slight fall so rain runs towards the beds rather than the house. Skipping the sub-base is the classic cause of sinking and rocking slabs.
- **Upkeep is modest but real.** Shady paving can go green with algae and moss and needs an occasional wash. Some natural stones are porous and prone to staining, so check before you buy.
- **Slip resistance varies.** Smooth, polished surfaces are the slipperiest when wet. Ask for the slip rating, or choose a textured finish for shady, damp spots.
- **Drainage depends on the system.** Standard paving sheds water, so it needs a fall and somewhere for water to go. Permeable systems let rain soak through to the ground below.

## When Does Decking Make Sense?

A deck suits sites where digging out and laying a base would be hard work, such as sloping ground or a raised level at the back door. Timber is warm underfoot and forgiving on bare feet, and it looks wonderful beside planting.

- **Damp is the enemy.** According to Savoy Timber, algae and moss thrive on moist timber, and standing water makes boards slippery, with a hidden ice risk in cold weather. Grooved boards, good airflow beneath and sweeping standing water away all help.
- **Treatment is ongoing.** The same source notes that decking stains can hinder algae but need reapplying yearly. Composite boards cut some of this upkeep, though they still need cleaning.
- **Rot starts where moisture lingers.** Clemson Cooperative Extension explains that decay becomes an important factor when timber meets soil and moisture, which is why airflow under the structure matters as much as the boards.
- **It still needs foundations.** A deck needs solid footings and a level frame. It's not "no foundations", just different ones.

At its best, decking sits in a sunny, well-drained spot. At its most demanding, it sits in a permanently damp corner.

## Is Gravel Good Enough for Paths and Informal Areas?

Yes, and it's often the best choice there. Gravel (crushed stone, or decomposed granite in some regions) drains freely, is easy to shape around curves and trees, and lets rain soak straight into the ground.

- **Choose the right grade.** The RHS suggests medium-grade angular gravel of around 20mm or larger over small rounded stones, as it's easier to walk on, and gives a rough coverage figure of about 0.6 square metres per 25kg bag at 5cm depth.
- **Weeds appear.** Weeding is heaviest in the first few years, and you'll need to top up the gravel over time.
- **Layers stop it disappearing.** Gardening Australia's gravel path method uses edging, a permeable membrane, a thin sand layer, then about 5cm of gravel, to stop it sinking and hold it in place.
- **Not for everyone.** Gravel can be awkward for wheelchairs, pushchairs and wheeled bins, and it migrates downhill on slopes.
- **Heavy, damp ground.** The RHS warns that gravel gardens struggle in heavy, clay-based, damp soils, so a waterlogged garden needs drainage addressed first. See [what to do with a boggy garden](/notes/boggy-waterlogged-garden-what-to-do).

## How Do They Compare Side by Side?

These are general rules of thumb rather than measured figures, and your site, materials and installer will move them.

| | Paving | Decking | Gravel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lifespan** | Generally longest, if the base is right | Generally shorter; depends on timber and care | Long-lived, needs topping up |
| **Upkeep** | Occasional wash; algae in shade | Regular cleaning and treatment | Weeding, raking, top-ups |
| **Slip risk when wet** | Varies with finish | Higher if algae builds up | Low |
| **Drainage** | Needs a fall (or permeable system) | Water falls through gaps | Excellent |
| **Slopes** | Needs terracing | Often the easiest option | Tends to migrate |
| **Wet garden** | Fine with drainage | Most at risk | Needs drainage fixed first |
| **Small courtyard** | Very well suited | Good if sunny | Works, but crunchy underfoot |
| **DIY difficulty** | Moderate to hard | Moderate | Easiest |
| **Relative cost** | Mid to highest | Lowest to mid | Lowest |

The cost row is only a relative guide. For real numbers see our [garden design cost breakdown](/notes/garden-design-cost-breakdown-2026).

## Which Surface Suits My Type of Garden?

- **A sloping garden:** decking often copes best, with paved terraces next. Gravel works for gentle gradients only. See [landscaping a sloped garden](/notes/landscaping-a-sloped-garden).
- **A wet or shady garden:** paving with a proper fall, or a permeable system. Be cautious with timber decking.
- **A small courtyard:** paving gives the cleanest, most stable result, with a little gravel between stepping stones to soften it.
- **You want low upkeep:** paving or composite decking, kept clear of leaf litter. No surface is zero maintenance.
- **Tight budget or DIY:** gravel paths, or a small deck. Our guide on [DIY or hire a landscaper](/notes/diy-or-hire-a-landscaper) helps you decide where to spend.
- **You want a mix:** most good gardens combine two. Paved seating, gravel paths, and a deck where levels change.

## By region

### England

Hard surfaces in a front garden are regulated. GOV.UK's guidance says that if you cover more than 5 square metres, planning permission is needed for traditional impermeable surfacing unless rainwater runs to a lawn or border, or the surface is permeable. Gravel, porous asphalt, permeable block paving and rain gardens are named as ways to meet this. Stricter rules apply in conservation areas, National Parks and similar designations. Back gardens are generally less restricted, but check your own property.

### Scotland

mygov.scot says most hard surfaces are permitted development, unless the surface sits between your house and a road, or you are in a conservation area or near a listed building. A front surface must be porous or drain to a porous area such as grass or a border. For decking, the same service sets permitted-development limits, including a floor height no higher than 0.5 metres above ground level at the back of the house, so a raised deck needs a conversation with your council.

### Wales

The Welsh Government says new hard surfaces in a front garden should use permeable or porous materials, or drain to a permeable surface, and repairing up to 5 square metres of existing hard surface is excepted. Planning permission is generally not needed, though significant embanking or terracing to support a surface might need an application. Welsh rules differ from England's, as the Planning Portal itself notes.

### Northern Ireland

We couldn't find a clear, current page covering garden hard surfaces, so we won't guess. Ask your local council's planning office before laying a large front-garden surface or building a raised deck.

### Ireland

Waterford City and County Council's exempted-development guidance says that for front and side gardens, hard surfaces of 25 square metres or more, or covering more than half the garden, should be built with permeable materials or otherwise let rainwater soak into the ground. New or widened road access generally needs permission. Practice varies between councils, so confirm with yours.

### United States

Climate drives the choice more than rules do. In freeze-thaw regions, Oregon State University Extension reports that properly designed porous pavements perform well, because the open structure leaves room for water to expand without damaging the surface. In the humid South, decay is the main worry for timber: Clemson's guidance (written for South Carolina) stresses that decay sets in where wood meets moisture and soil, and separates lumber suited to ground contact from lumber that isn't. That favours well-ventilated, above-ground deck framing, or paving. We haven't sourced desert-heat guidance, so check local suppliers for how timber and dark paving cope with intense sun.

### Australia

Check your Bushfire Attack Level (BAL) first. NSW Rural Fire Service explains that BAL measures potential exposure to embers, radiant heat and flame, and that unenclosed decks on bushfire-prone land must use materials more resistant to ignition. Wood Solutions' guide says standard treated pine is acceptable for BAL-LOW to BAL-19 beyond 300mm from glazing, while BAL-29 calls for bushfire-resisting timbers such as spotted gum or blackbutt, or non-combustible materials. In a high-risk zone, paving or gravel can be the simpler answer. The same guide recommends termite protection at ground level and keeping garden beds away from the house, so ask your builder about termite management for timber near the ground.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which is cheaper: gravel, decking or paving?

Gravel is generally the cheapest, decking sits in the lower to middle range and paving runs from mid to highest. Materials, access and labour vary, so get several quotes for your own garden.

### Is decking more slippery than paving?

It can be, if algae and moss build up on damp timber. Grooved boards, airflow and regular cleaning reduce the risk. Smooth paving can be slippery when wet too, so check slip ratings.

### Do I need planning permission for paving?

Often not, but front gardens are the exception. In England, covering more than 5 square metres with impermeable material needs permission unless water drains to a border or lawn. Scotland and Wales ask for permeable or draining surfaces too. Check with your local authority.

### Can I put gravel on a slope?

Only a gentle one. On steeper ground gravel migrates downhill, so you'd need edging and stepped sections, or a different surface altogether.

### Do I really need a sub-base for a patio?

Yes. A compacted, well-prepared base keeps paving level for years, and it's the part of the job most worth getting right.
