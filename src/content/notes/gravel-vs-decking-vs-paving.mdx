<Callout type="note">
  **Quick answer.** There is no best surface, only the best one for your ground, your climate and how much upkeep you'll tolerate. Paving (pavers) is the sturdiest and most formal, decking (a deck) is the quickest way to level an awkward spot but needs regular care in damp conditions, and gravel (crushed stone) is the most forgiving on drainage but the least comfortable underfoot.
</Callout>

Most people choose a garden surface the way they choose a sofa: by how it looks in a photo. That's a fine place to start, but it's the wrong place to finish. The surface you pick decides how the garden handles rain, how it feels in January, and how many Saturdays you'll spend scrubbing it. Here's a fair comparison of the three.

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture a shady patch at the back of the house. You love the look of a warm timber deck, so you build one. Two winters later it's green and slick by the back door, and you're wondering whether you chose wrong. Or you pick pale paving, and discover that the corner where water pools now has a permanent puddle. Neither is a design failure exactly. Each surface simply met a condition it wasn't suited to.

That's the idea behind this whole comparison. Match the surface to the conditions and any of the three can work beautifully. If you're still weighing the bigger picture, our note on [hard landscaping vs soft landscaping](/notes/hard-landscaping-vs-soft-landscaping) sets out how surfaces fit with planting.

## Paving: Solid, Durable, and Only as Good as Its Base

Paving (pavers, in the US and Australia) covers everything from natural stone to porcelain and concrete block. It's the longest-lived of the three and gives you the flattest, most stable surface for furniture, which is why it's the usual choice for a main seating area.

**What to know:**

- **Base is everything.** Gardening Australia's presenter puts it plainly: pavers are only as good as the base. Their worked example uses a compacted road base layer, a sand bed, then the pavers, compacting at each stage, with a slight fall so rain runs towards the beds rather than the house. Skipping the sub-base is the classic cause of sinking and rocking slabs.
- **Maintenance is modest but real.** Shady paving can go green with algae and moss, and needs an occasional wash. Some natural stones are porous and prone to staining, so check before you buy.
- **Slip resistance varies by material.** Smooth, polished surfaces are the slipperiest when wet. Ask for the slip rating, or choose a textured finish for shady, damp spots.
- **Drainage depends on the system.** Standard paving sheds water, so it needs a fall and somewhere for water to go. Permeable systems use an open-graded sub-base so rain can soak through.

A note on rules. In some places, laying large impermeable areas, particularly in front gardens, is restricted or needs permission, precisely because hard surfaces add to surface-water flooding. In England, for instance, paving a front garden over five square metres with impermeable material needs planning permission unless rainwater drains to a lawn or border, or the surface is permeable. Rules differ between countries and councils, so check locally before you buy.

## Decking: Warm, Quick, and Hungry for Attention

A deck (decking) suits sites where digging out and laying a base would be hard work, such as sloping ground, or a raised level at the back door. Timber is warm underfoot and forgiving on bare feet, and it can look wonderful beside planting.

**What to know:**

- **Damp is the enemy.** According to Savoy Timber, algae and moss thrive on moist timber, and standing water makes boards slippery, with a hidden ice risk in cold weather. Grooved boards, good airflow beneath and sweeping standing water away all help.
- **Treatment is ongoing.** The same source notes that decking stains can hinder algae but need reapplying yearly. Composite boards cut some of this upkeep, though they still need cleaning.
- **Rot comes from below.** Airflow under the structure matters as much as the boards. A deck built tight to the ground in a damp, shady spot will age faster.
- **Substructure matters too.** A deck still needs solid footings and a level frame. It's not "no foundations", just different ones.

Decking is generally at its best in a sunny, well-drained spot, and at its most demanding in a permanently damp corner.

## Gravel: Easy on Drainage, Honest About Its Limits

Gravel (crushed stone, or decomposed granite in some regions) is the flexible one. It drains freely, it's easy to shape around curves and trees, and it lets rain soak straight into the ground.

**What to know:**

- **Choose the right grade.** The RHS suggests medium-grade angular gravel of around 20mm or larger over small rounded stones, as it's easier to walk on, and gives a rough coverage figure of about 0.6 square metres per 25kg bag at 5cm depth.
- **Weeds appear.** Weeding is heaviest in the first few years, and you'll need to top up the gravel over time.
- **Layers stop it disappearing.** Gardening Australia's gravel path method uses edging, a permeable membrane, a thin sand layer, then about 5cm of gravel, to stop it sinking into the soil and to hold it in place.
- **Not ideal for everyone.** Gravel can be awkward for wheelchairs, pushchairs and wheeled bins, and tricky on slopes where it migrates downhill. Edging helps, but it isn't magic.
- **Heavy, damp ground.** The RHS warns that gravel gardens struggle in heavy, clay-based, damp soils, so a waterlogged garden needs drainage addressed first. See [what to do with a boggy garden](/notes/boggy-waterlogged-garden-what-to-do).

Gravel is also the surface where you can most easily DIY a path, and the easiest to change your mind about later.

## Side by Side

| | Paving | Decking | Gravel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lifespan** | Longest, if the base is right | Shorter; depends on timber and care | Long-lived, needs topping up |
| **Upkeep** | Occasional wash; algae in shade | Regular cleaning and treatment | Weeding, raking, top-ups |
| **Slip risk when wet** | Varies with finish | Higher if algae builds up | Low |
| **Drainage** | Needs a fall (or permeable system) | Water falls through gaps | Excellent |
| **Slopes** | Needs terracing | Often the easiest option | Tends to migrate |
| **Wet garden** | Fine with drainage | Most at risk | Needs drainage fixed first |
| **Small courtyard** | Very well suited | Good if sunny | Works, but crunchy underfoot |
| **DIY difficulty** | Moderate to hard | Moderate | Easiest |
| **Relative cost** | Mid to highest | Lowest to mid | Lowest |

## Which One Is Right for You?

- **A sloping garden:** decking often copes best, with paved terraces next. Gravel works for gentle gradients only. See [landscaping a sloped garden](/notes/landscaping-a-sloped-garden).
- **A wet or shady garden:** paving with a proper fall, or a permeable system. Be cautious with timber decking.
- **A small courtyard:** paving gives the cleanest, most stable result. A little gravel between stepping stones softens it.
- **You want low upkeep:** paving or composite decking, kept clear of leaf litter. Remember that no surface is zero maintenance.
- **You're on a tight budget or doing it yourself:** gravel paths, or a small deck. Our guide on [DIY or hire a landscaper](/notes/diy-or-hire-a-landscaper) helps you decide where to spend.
- **You want a mix:** most good gardens combine two. Paved seating, gravel paths, and a deck where levels change.

If you do hire help, [what to ask before you hire a landscaper](/notes/before-you-hire-a-landscaper) will help you compare quotes fairly, particularly on how the base will be built.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which is cheaper: gravel, decking or paving?

Generally gravel is the lowest cost, decking sits in the lower to middle range and paving runs from mid to highest, but it depends on materials and site access. Get several quotes for your own garden.

### Is decking more slippery than paving?

It can be, if algae and moss are allowed to build up on damp timber. Grooved boards, airflow and regular cleaning reduce the risk. Smooth paving can be slippery when wet too, so check slip ratings.

### Do I need planning permission for paving?

It depends where you live and which part of the garden it is. In England, paving a front garden with impermeable material over five square metres needs permission unless water drains to a border or lawn. Check with your local authority.

### Can I put gravel on a slope?

Only gentle ones. On steeper ground gravel migrates downhill, so you'd need edging and stepped sections, or a different surface altogether.

### Do I really need a sub-base for a patio?

Yes. A compacted, well-prepared base is what keeps paving level for years. It's the part of the job most worth getting right.
