Picture a warm, bright Saturday in spring. The garden centre is full, the bedding plants look wonderful, and you plant a dozen out that afternoon. Then comes one clear, still night, and by breakfast they're black and limp.

Nothing was wrong with the plants, or with you. The weather felt like spring, but the frost risk hadn't passed. Knowing roughly when it drops away, for your part of the world, is the difference between a spring that starts well and one that starts with replanting.

Our guides to [hardiness zones in Ireland](/notes/plant-hardiness-zones-ireland-beginners-guide) and [the UK's planting zones](/notes/what-planting-zone-is-the-uk-in) cover which plants survive your winter. Frost dates tell you when it's safe to start the season.

## What Does "Last Frost Date" Actually Mean?

A last frost date is the typical final date in spring when frost occurs at your location. It is worked out from historical weather records, so it describes what has usually happened, not what will happen this year.

The key idea is that it comes with a probability attached. In the US, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) shows the date after which the chance of freezing temperatures falls below 50 per cent, and its Climate Normals also give dates for the 40, 30, 20 and 10 per cent thresholds. NOAA notes that gardeners wanting higher confidence for frost-sensitive crops can use the 10 per cent date.

The National Weather Service's Medford, Oregon example shows how this works. Plant frost-sensitive vegetables on 16 April and there is a 90 per cent chance of at least one more morning at 36°F or lower. By 5 May it's an even chance, and by 22 May only a 10 per cent chance remains. It suggests low-tolerance gardeners wait for the later date.

Some popular tools use other thresholds. The Old Farmer's Almanac uses a 30 per cent threshold and reminds readers that frost remains possible after the listed date. Check which probability sits behind a date before you trust it.

### Is frost the same as ground frost?

Not quite. The Met Office explains that an air frost is when air temperature, measured about a metre up, falls to freezing or below. A ground frost is when surfaces such as grass and leaves fall below 0°C, even if the air stays above freezing.

Your plants live at ground level, so the forecast can say 2°C and you can still get frost on the leaves. Australia's Bureau of Meteorology makes the same point: it uses an air temperature of 3°C or below as an indicator of frost, because the thermometer sits slightly above the ground.

## How Do I Find My Own Date?

How you find your date depends on where you garden.

**In the US,** use NOAA's [last freeze map](https://www.climate.gov/LastFreeze) for the 50 per cent date, or the lower-probability Climate Normals dates, and cross-check with your state or county extension service.

**In the UK and Ireland,** exact "last frost date" tables are much less standard than in North America, so most gardeners use regional rules of thumb. The Royal Horticultural Society (RHS) gives approximate safe windows for planting out tender plants, set out in the next section.

**In Australia,** use the Bureau of Meteorology's description of your local frost season and its frost warnings, then adjust for your own low spots and high ground.

## By region

The right date genuinely changes with where you are. These are the sourced rules of thumb, with nothing invented to fill gaps.

### South-west and south coast England

The RHS puts the safe window for planting out tender plants at the first to second half of May. This is the earliest of any UK region, thanks to milder, more maritime conditions.

### Southern England and south Wales

The RHS suggests the second half of May.

### Midlands, north-west England, Yorkshire and north Wales

The RHS suggests the end of May. The Midlands is the example we use most: sow tender seeds indoors so seedlings are sturdy by then, and begin hardening off about three weeks before.

### North-east England, southern Scotland and Northern Ireland

The RHS suggests early June.

### Highlands and northern Scotland

The RHS suggests mid June. Hardy plants will still go out far earlier; it is the tender ones that wait.

The RHS adds that sheltered urban or coastal spots may allow planting a week or two earlier, while exposed or higher sites may need one to three weeks longer. It also advises night-time temperatures above 7°C before planting out, and above 10°C for heat-lovers such as tomatoes, peppers, basil and cucurbits.

### Ireland: coastal versus inland

We could not find a published Irish last-frost table, so we won't invent one. What Met Éireann does say is that air frost is "not infrequent" at inland locations even in May. As a working default, treat the RHS window for the nearest part of Britain as a starting point, and add caution for inland and higher gardens, where the cold lingers longer than on the coast.

### US: Pacific Northwest

The University of Washington Botanic Gardens puts Seattle's average last frost date at 20 April. Medford in southern Oregon sits later: NWS data there show a 50 per cent chance of another frost on 5 May, falling to 10 per cent by 22 May. Two Pacific Northwest places, two quite different dates.

### US: Midwest, Plains and East Coast cities

Analysis of NOAA's 1991-2020 normals, reported by The Weather Channel, puts the last freeze between about 10 and 30 April for the Plains, Midwest and East Coast cities. The same source notes that Chicago's last freeze has come as late as 25 May (in 1992), a reminder that averages are not guarantees.

### US: Mountain West and the northern tier

The northern tier and Mountain West typically see the last freeze between about 30 April and 15 May. NOAA's example is Boulder, Colorado, where the 50 per cent date is around 6 May and the 10 per cent date around 18 May. Near the Canadian border and at the highest Rocky Mountain elevations it can come after 15 May.

### US: South, Gulf Coast and California

The same analysis puts the last freeze before 1 March for the Gulf Coast and Deep South cities, and for the Los Angeles and San Diego area, which rarely freeze. Large parts of the wider South, the Interstate 95 corridor and sections of the Desert Southwest and West Coast fall between about 1 and 30 March.

### Australia: southern and central states

The Bureau of Meteorology says frost is most common in southern and central Australia. At low elevations on the mainland it can generally occur from late autumn to early spring, while the frost season is much longer at higher elevations and in Tasmania. In practical terms, tender plants in Victoria, South Australia and Tasmania need a more cautious start to spring than their calendar suggests. The Bureau issues frost warnings for those three states, and it singles out frost hollows, low-lying pockets where cold air pools, as especially prone.

<Callout type="tip">
  **Gardening in the southern hemisphere?** The logic is the same with the months reversed. We haven't quoted a single national date range because the Bureau describes the frost season rather than a last-frost date, so use your local frost season and the forecast.
</Callout>

## Why Is It a Probability and Not a Promise?

A late cold snap can follow any date. Around the 50 per cent point you're accepting a coin-flip on frost, which is fine for hardy plants or with fleece to hand. Around the 10 per cent point, or the end of your region's RHS range, little risk remains, though never zero.

Your own tolerance matters too. A packet of seed can be resown; a large tender specimen can't, so the more you stand to lose, the later your date should be.

## How Do I Turn the Date Into a Sowing Plan?

Sorting plants into three groups makes it simple.

**Hardy plants** cope with frost and can go out early. **Half-hardy plants** tolerate cool weather but not frost, so they wait for your last frost date, ideally with a margin. **Tender plants** are damaged by cold even above freezing and need the longest wait, or a warm windowsill, such as tomatoes.

Check the packet or label for the group a plant falls into; our guide to [what plant label symbols mean](/notes/what-do-plant-label-symbols-mean) helps, and [RHS hardiness versus USDA zones](/notes/rhs-hardiness-vs-usda-zones) explains how the rating systems relate.

Plan with a target date and a "not before" date, and let the forecast decide between them. If a clear, still night is forecast just before your planned day, wait. If your garden is a sheltered town suntrap, you might go a week earlier; on a wind-scoured plot, add a week or two.

## Should I Harden Off, and What About Fleece?

Plants raised under cover are used to still, warm conditions, so moving them straight into spring wind is a shock. The RHS describes hardening off as taking two to three weeks: a cold greenhouse in week one, a cold frame with the lid opened a little more each day in week two, then the lid off in week three. With no greenhouse, use a sheltered south-facing spot under two layers of fleece, bringing plants in at night at first and reducing the cover over a fortnight.

One point is worth underlining: hardening off toughens leaves, but it does not make frost-tender plants hardy. It prepares them for cool, windy conditions, not for frost.

For frost itself, keep **fleece** to hand. The RHS notes heavier grades give about 2°C of protection, with a double layer on the coldest nights. **Cloches** and cold frames work similarly for smaller plants. Treat them as insurance, not a licence to plant weeks early.

## How Does My Microclimate Shift the Date?

A regional date is an average of many gardens, and yours can shift a week or two either way.

Cold air is heavy and flows downhill, pooling in hollows and at the bottom of slopes. The Bureau of Meteorology calls these frost hollows, and the RHS advises avoiding frost pockets for tender plants or planting later there. Walls and hard surfaces store daytime warmth and release it at night, so beds beside them often stay frost-free longer. Cloud also matters: the Met Office describes it as acting like a blanket, while calm, clear nights let heat escape fastest.

In practice the corner by the south-facing wall may be ready first and the low spot last. A dawn walk on a frosty morning shows where the white lingers; our note on [microclimates in your garden](/notes/microclimates-in-your-garden) covers how to read these differences.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a last frost date?

It's the typical final date in spring when frost occurs at your location, based on historical records. It comes with a probability, such as 50 or 10 per cent, so frost can still happen after it.

### When can I plant out tender plants in the UK?

From the second half of May in southern England to the end of May in the Midlands and north of England, and early to mid June in Scotland, according to the RHS. Wait for nights above 7°C.

### Is the last frost date the same every year?

No. It's an average, and individual springs vary, so checking the forecast a week ahead matters more than the calendar date.

### Can I plant before my last frost date?

Yes, if the plants are hardy, or if you can protect them with fleece, a cloche or a cold frame. For frost-tender plants, wait until the risk has passed.

### Do I need a last frost date in Ireland?

Not an exact one. Use the nearest RHS regional window as a starting point, and be more cautious inland, where Met Éireann notes air frost is not infrequent even in May.
