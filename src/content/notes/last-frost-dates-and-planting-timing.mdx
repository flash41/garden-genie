January is when we start thinking about spring, and "when can I plant this out?" is a question we hear constantly. The honest answer is a date range and a bit of judgement. This guide explains what a last frost date is, how to find a useful one, and how to turn it into a sowing and planting rhythm you can trust.

---

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture a warm, bright Saturday in spring. The garden centre is full, the bedding plants look wonderful, and you plant a dozen out that afternoon. Then comes one clear, still night, and by breakfast they're black and limp.

Nothing was wrong with the plants, or with you. The weather felt like spring, but the frost risk hadn't passed. Knowing roughly when it drops away is the difference between a spring that starts well and one that starts with replanting.

Our guides to [hardiness zones in Ireland](/notes/plant-hardiness-zones-ireland-beginners-guide) and [the UK's planting zones](/notes/what-planting-zone-is-the-uk-in) cover which plants survive your winter. Frost dates tell you when it's safe to start the season.

## What a "Last Frost Date" Actually Means

A last frost date is the typical final date in spring when frost occurs at your location. It is worked out from historical weather records, so it describes what has usually happened, not what will happen this year.

The key idea is that it comes with a probability attached. In the US, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) publishes the average date after which the chance of freezing temperatures falls below 50 per cent, and its Climate Normals also give dates for lower thresholds of 40, 30, 20 and 10 per cent. NOAA notes that gardeners wanting higher confidence for frost-sensitive crops can use the 10 per cent date.

The National Weather Service's Medford, Oregon example shows how this works: at the 90 per cent date another frost is very likely, at 50 per cent it's an even chance, and at 10 per cent only a small chance remains. It suggests low-tolerance gardeners wait for the 10 per cent date.

Some popular tools use other thresholds; the Old Farmer's Almanac, for instance, uses a 30 per cent threshold and reminds readers that frost remains possible after the listed date. Check which probability sits behind a date before you trust it.

### Frost, air frost and ground frost

It also helps to know that "frost" isn't one thing. The Met Office explains that an air frost is when air temperature, measured about a metre up, falls to freezing or below. A ground frost is when surfaces such as soil and leaves fall below 0°C, and it can happen without an air frost.

Your plants live at ground level, so the forecast can say 2°C and you can still get frost on the leaves. Australia's Bureau of Meteorology makes the same point: its forecasts flag frost at minimums of 3°C or below, because the thermometer sits slightly above the ground.

## How to Find Yours (and What to Do If There Isn't One)

How you find your date depends on where you garden.

**In the US.** Use NOAA's [last freeze map](https://www.climate.gov/LastFreeze) for the 50 per cent date, or the lower-probability Climate Normals dates, and cross-check with your state or county extension service.

**In Australia.** The Bureau of Meteorology says frost in southern and central Australia typically occurs from late autumn to early spring, and that frost hollows, where cold air pools in low spots, are especially prone. Its frost warnings for Tasmania, Victoria and South Australia, add useful detail.

**In the UK and Ireland.** This is where we want to be straight with you. Exact "last frost date" tables are much less standard here than in North America, and most gardeners use regional rules of thumb instead. The Royal Horticultural Society (RHS) gives approximate safe windows for planting out tender plants, which we've set out below. Ireland's national weather service, Met Éireann, notes that air frost is "not infrequent" at inland locations even in May, which is a good reminder that the coast and the inland midlands can behave quite differently.

| Region (RHS guidance) | Approximate planting-out window for tender plants |
|---|---|
| South-west and south coast England | First to second half of May |
| Southern England and south Wales | Second half of May |
| Midlands, north-west England, Yorkshire, north Wales | End of May |
| North-east England, southern Scotland, Northern Ireland | Early June |
| Highlands and northern Scotland | Mid-June |

The RHS adds that sheltered urban or coastal spots may allow planting a week or two earlier, while exposed or higher sites may need one to three weeks longer. For the Republic of Ireland, treat the nearest region as a starting point and adjust for your site.

<Callout type="tip">
  **A note on months.** Everything above is written for the northern hemisphere. If you garden in Australia, New Zealand or South Africa, the calendar flips: the last frosts of the season tend to fall in late winter to spring, roughly August to October depending on where you are, and "spring planting" means the same logic applied to those months.
</Callout>

## Why It's a Probability, Not a Promise

A late cold snap can follow any date. The RHS recommends checking the 5 to 10 day forecast before finalising planting, with night-time temperatures above 7°C before planting out tender plants, and heat-loving crops such as tomatoes happier above 10°C.

Around the 50 per cent point you're accepting a coin-flip on frost, fine for hardy plants or with fleece to hand. Around the 10 per cent point, or the end of your region's RHS range, little risk remains, though never zero.

Your own tolerance matters too. A packet of seed can be resown; a large tender specimen can't, so the more you stand to lose, the later your date should be.

## Turning the Date Into a Sowing and Planting Plan

Once you have a working date, plant types give you a simple structure. We find it easiest to sort things into three groups.

**Hardy plants** cope with frost and can go out early. **Half-hardy plants** tolerate cool weather but not frost, so they wait for your last frost date, ideally with a margin. **Tender plants** are damaged by cold even above freezing and need the warmest wait, or a heated windowsill, such as tomatoes.

Check the packet or label for the group a plant falls into; our guide to [what plant label symbols mean](/notes/what-do-plant-label-symbols-mean) helps, and [RHS hardiness versus USDA zones](/notes/rhs-hardiness-vs-usda-zones) explains how the rating systems relate.

### A worked example in plain language

Say you garden in the English Midlands, where the RHS suggests the end of May for planting out tender plants. You sow tender seeds indoors, following the packet, so seedlings are sturdy by then, and start hardening off three weeks before. If a clear, still night is forecast just before your planned day, you wait. If your garden is a sheltered town suntrap, you might go a week earlier; on a wind-scoured plot, add a week or two. Plan with a target date and a "not before" date, and let the forecast decide between them.

## Hardening Off, Fleece and Cloches

Plants raised under cover are used to still, warm conditions, so moving them straight into spring wind is a shock. Hardening off is the fix. The RHS describes two to three weeks: a cold greenhouse in week one, a cold frame with the lid opened a little more each day in week two, then the lid off in week three. With no greenhouse, use a sheltered south-facing spot under two layers of fleece, bringing plants in at night at first and reducing the cover over a fortnight.

One point from the RHS is worth underlining: hardening off thickens and toughens leaves, but it does not make frost-tender plants hardy. It prepares them for cool, windy conditions. It won't save them from frost.

For frost itself, keep **fleece** to hand. The RHS notes heavier grades give around 2°C of protection, with a double layer adding more on severe nights. **Cloches** and cold frames work similarly for smaller plants. Treat them as insurance, not a licence to plant weeks early.

## How Your Microclimate Shifts the Date

A regional date is an average of many gardens, and yours can shift a week or two either way.

Cold air is heavy and flows downhill, pooling in hollows and at the bottom of slopes. The Bureau of Meteorology calls these frost hollows, and the RHS advises avoiding frost pockets entirely for tender plants or planting later there. Walls, buildings and hard surfaces store daytime warmth and release it at night, so beds beside them often stay frost-free longer. Cloud cover, wind and open sky also matter: the Met Office points out that patchy cloud acts like a blanket, while a clear, still night lets heat escape quickly.

In practice the corner by the south-facing wall may be ready first and the low spot last. A dawn walk on a frosty morning shows where the white lingers; our note on [microclimates in your garden](/notes/microclimates-in-your-garden) covers how to read these differences.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a last frost date?

It's the typical final date in spring when frost occurs at your location, based on historical records. It comes with a probability, such as 50 or 10 per cent, so frost can still happen after it.

### Is the last frost date the same every year?

No. It's an average, and individual springs vary, so checking the forecast a week ahead matters more than the calendar date.

### Do I need a last frost date in the UK or Ireland?

Not an exact one. Most gardeners use regional rules of thumb, such as the RHS planting-out windows, adjusted for their own site.

### Can I plant before my last frost date?

Yes, if the plants are hardy, or if you can protect them with fleece, a cloche or a cold frame. For frost-tender plants, wait until the risk has passed.

### How does the last frost date work in the southern hemisphere?

The logic is the same with the months reversed: last frosts tend to fall in late winter to spring, roughly August to October for many gardens.
