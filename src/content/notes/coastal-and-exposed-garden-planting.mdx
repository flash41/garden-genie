<Callout type="note">
  **Quick answer.** Plant tough, salt-tolerant shrubs and hedging such as sea buckthorn, hawthorn, rugosa rose or coastal natives along the front line, behind a permeable windbreak that filters the wind rather than blocking it. Then tuck softer plants into the shelter that creates. Wind and salt spray usually decide what survives, more than the winter minimum on a plant label.
</Callout>

If your garden sits on a headland, a hillside or a gust-funnelling estate edge, you may have noticed that the usual planting advice doesn't quite fit. The plant is "hardy", the soil looks fine, and yet the leaves go brown at the edges by midsummer and the whole thing leans like it's walking into a gale. This guide explains why, and how to plant so the wind works with you.

---

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture the scenario. You move to a house a few minutes from the sea, or to a bare hilltop plot with a view and no neighbours to break the weather. You buy a handsome hydrangea and a few lovely perennials, tuck them into a border, and enjoy them for about six weeks. Then a spring gale arrives, the leaves crisp and curl, and you assume you've done something wrong.

You haven't. You've met the real constraint of an exposed site. Wind dries plants out faster than their roots can replace the water, and on the coast it often carries salt as well. Neither shows up in a hardiness rating, which is why we always say a zone number is only the start of the conversation. If you haven't looked at yours yet, our guides to [UK hardiness zones](/notes/what-planting-zone-is-the-uk-in) and [Irish hardiness zones](/notes/plant-hardiness-zones-ireland-beginners-guide) are a good place to begin, and [microclimates in your garden](/notes/microclimates-in-your-garden) explains why one corner can behave so differently from the next.

## Why Do Wind and Salt Matter More Than Hardiness?

The Royal Horticultural Society describes the main challenge of a seaside garden as blustery wind and salty sea spray that dry plants out quickly ([RHS, Coastal plants](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/articles/misc/coastal-plants)). University of Massachusetts guidance lists a similar set for the US east coast: salt spray, wind, poor soil, dry conditions, and sometimes shifting sand or saltwater overwash ([UMass Extension](https://www.umass.edu/agriculture-food-environment/landscape/fact-sheets/selection-maintenance-of-plant-materials-for-coastal-landscapes)).

Australian writer Trevor Blake describes how to gauge your own exposure: ask whether you are in direct line with the destructive wind, and notice whether a greasy film builds up on windows after a storm. He also points out that rain arriving with those storms can wash salt off the leaves ([Blake, A Garden and a Puff of Salt Air](https://anpsa.org.au/APOL13/mar99-5.html)).

Three practical takeaways:

- **Exposure is not the same as cold.** Many coastal gardens enjoy mild winters, so frost-tender plants can sometimes be tried there, but the wind can still scorch them. An exposed hill garden may be colder and windier with no salt at all.
- **Distance matters, but not in a neat line.** Florida's extension service suggests treating plants within roughly an eighth of a mile of salt water as needing at least some salt tolerance ([UF/IFAS](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/design/landscaping-for-specific-sites/salt-tolerant-plants/)). Your garden may be better or worse depending on the prevailing wind and the land around it.
- **Look at what's already surviving.** Hedges, hawthorns and wild coastal species that look healthy nearby are free advice.

## What Is the Best Windbreak for a Coastal Garden?

The best windbreak is a permeable one. A solid fence or wall feels like the obvious answer, but the RHS advises that a windbreak should filter roughly half to 60 percent of the wind, because a solid barrier creates damaging eddies on both sides ([RHS, Windbreaks and shelterbelts](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/hedges/windbreaks-shelterbelts)). Woven willow hurdles, slatted fencing and a good hedge all do this well.

The same page says a windbreak reduces wind on its sheltered side for a distance of around ten times its height, and that it should be wide enough, or have returns at the ends, so the wind doesn't simply slip round the sides. A 1.5 metre barrier is therefore doing useful work for a good stretch of garden behind it.

A few design ideas we've found work well in exposed gardens:

- **Layer it.** UMass suggests low, tough plants on the exposed front line, such as rugosa rose or bayberry, with taller evergreens behind in a staggered double row, rising in height away from the wind.
- **Use a hedge instead of a wall where you can.** Blake notes that dense hedges or screens widen your plant choices dramatically.
- **Plant small.** The RHS recommends small, young plants for shelter belts because they establish better than large ones. Mulch them and water through the first dry spells.
- **Keep the view.** A permeable screen can frame a sea view rather than hide it.

## Which Salt-Tolerant Plants Should You Choose?

Rather than a long list, think about the job each plant does. These examples come from the sources below; ask a local nursery which varieties they know cope with salt. Blake is blunt on this point: check where a plant was sourced, because coastal species grown from inland stock may not tolerate salt as well.

**The front line (shelter belt and hedge).** The RHS windbreak list includes *Elaeagnus × ebbingei*, yew, hawthorn, hazel and sea buckthorn, and flags hawthorn, sea buckthorn and willows as coping with salt-laden coastal winds. UMass points to rugosa rose and beach plum, among others.

**Soft, silvery fillers.** The RHS notes that plants with narrow, grass-like or needle-like foliage, or silvery, leathery or waxy leaves, tend to cope well. That points to rosemary, sage and thyme, thrift (*Armeria*) and sea holly (*Eryngium*). UMass also lists English lavender among its non-native options.

**Structure and drama.** The RHS suggests tender exotics like bottlebrush and agave can thrive by the sea even though they struggle inland. If you're in a mild coastal pocket, this is where a warm, sheltered spot can pay off.

**Ground cover and grasses.** Native beach grasses and sedges suit sandy, windy ground (UMass), as do muhly grass and gaillardia on warmer coasts (UF/IFAS).

Salt tolerance is a spectrum, not a yes or no. Even tough plants can look ragged in a bad gale, so plan a front line that is easy to trim or replace.

## How Do You Help Plants Settle Into Sandy Soil?

Seaside soil is often sandy or stony and drains quickly. The RHS says many seaside plants prefer these poor conditions and stay healthier there than in rich inland soil. Our piece on [clay versus sandy soil](/notes/clay-soil-vs-sandy-soil-what-will-grow) goes into this in more detail.

- **Improve the planting area, not the whole garden.** UMass recommends working in a generous layer of organic matter, at least three inches, to help sandy soil hold nutrients and moisture.
- **Mulch with gravel or pebbles.** The RHS suggests locally sourced pebbles and gravel as a mulch to hold moisture and suppress weeds, and it looks right in the setting.
- **Water the first season.** UF/IFAS puts it neatly: even with salt water all around, coastal plants need fresh water to thrive. Drought-tolerant plants still need watering until they've rooted in. Our guide to [drought-tolerant planting](/notes/drought-tolerant-water-wise-planting) covers the longer-term approach.

## How Should You Plant, Stake and Build in an Exposed Garden?

Exposed gardens reward a lower profile. Put the tallest, most wind-sensitive plants behind a hedge or wall, keep the front in mounds, mats and clipped shapes, and leave the open space to tough grasses.

- **Protect new plants.** The RHS suggests shielding new plants from wind while they establish. Simple pebble or driftwood mini-windbreaks, or a temporary netting screen, can make the difference in year one.
- **Stake with restraint.** A short, low stake helps a young tree through its first seasons, but one held rigid never builds its own strength. Remove it once the roots have taken hold.
- **Choose hard landscaping for the weather.** In our experience, fixings and furniture near the sea tend to corrode, so favour stainless or well-protected metals, masonry, suitable timber and gravel. Check with your supplier what they recommend for salty air.

## By region

How hard the coast bites varies a good deal from place to place. The figures below come from the sources cited; treat them as a guide to the pattern rather than a forecast for your plot.

### South-west England and Cornwall

The Met Office describes south-west England as one of the UK's most wind-exposed regions, with coastal sites seeing roughly 16 to 24 gale days a year against 4 to 7 inland in Somerset. Winters are mild on the coast: the Isles of Scilly average fewer than two air frosts a year, while inland Somerset exceeds 50 ([Met Office, South West England climate](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/binaries/content/assets/metofficegovuk/pdf/weather/learn-about/uk-past-events/regional-climates/south-west-england_-climate---met-office.pdf)). That mildness lets you try tender silvery and architectural plants, but the wind is the real test, so shelter comes first.

### Western Scotland and the Hebrides

The west is milder than the east thanks to Atlantic influence, but the Met Office calls it one of the more exposed areas of the UK. Gale-force conditions occur about 5 days a year inland and over 25 in Hebridean locations ([Met Office, Western Scotland climate](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/binaries/content/assets/metofficegovuk/pdf/weather/learn-about/uk-past-events/regional-climates/western-scotland_-climate---met-office.pdf)). Here a deep, layered shelter belt matters more than any single clever plant.

### The east coast of England

Eastern England is described by the Met Office as one of the more sheltered parts of the UK, with coastal areas averaging about 5 gales a year and exposed Yorkshire and Humberside coasts around 10. Air frost ranges from under 30 days a year at the coast to about 55 well inland ([Met Office, Eastern England climate](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/binaries/content/assets/metofficegovuk/pdf/weather/learn-about/uk-past-events/regional-climates/eastern-england_-climate---met-office.pdf)). Wind is a smaller issue than in the west, so cold hardiness matters a little more when you choose between tender and tough plants.

### Ireland's Atlantic coast

Met Éireann says maritime influence is strongest near the Atlantic coasts, winters are cool and windy, and sea temperatures run warmer than land in winter, often giving rain at the coast but snow a few miles inland. Hills near the coast can provide shelter from strong winds ([Met Éireann, Climate of Ireland](https://www.met.ie/climate/climate-of-ireland)). In practice, a west-coast Irish garden can often use the same salt-tolerant hedging as Cornwall, with the same emphasis on filtering the westerly.

### US Pacific Northwest coast

Oregon State University Extension calls wind the biggest problem on the Oregon coast, with gusts of 45 to 65 mph, and describes conditions as moderate: not warm, but not very cold. The coast sits mostly in USDA zones 9a and 9b. Its suggested living windbreaks include escallonia, rhododendrons and euonymus ([OSU Extension, Coastal gardening](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/news/coastal-gardening-brings-challenges-rewards)).

### New England

On the Massachusetts coast the stressors are salt spray, wind, sandy soils and sometimes wave over-topping, and the state's coastal zone office recommends native species that need less watering and upkeep ([Mass. CZM, Fall planting on the coast](https://www.mass.gov/service-details/cz-tip-fall-planting-on-the-coast)). Its advice is to plant roughly six weeks before the first hard frost, generally September and October, so roots establish before the ground freezes. UMass adds rugosa rose, bayberry and beach plum for the exposed front.

### Florida and the Gulf coast

UF/IFAS advises that plants within about an eighth of a mile of salt water should be at least somewhat salt tolerant. Natives such as sea oats, muhly grass, gaillardia, live oak and cabbage palm are suggested, and some tropical choices belong only in zones 10 and 11 in the south of the state (UF/IFAS, Salt-Tolerant Plants). Note that "salt tolerant" ratings vary, and some plants do better a little inland.

### Southern Australia

The Kangaroo Island planting guide, from the South Australian government, reflects a coast of long, hot, dry summers with salt spray and sand-blasting, and separates plants for direct coastal exposure from those suited to sheltered backyards. Suggestions include drooping sheoak, common boobialla, native pigface and coast spear-grass, and many other species "do not tolerate direct coastal exposure" ([Kangaroo Island NRM Board, Coastal Gardens](https://cdn.environment.sa.gov.au/landscape/docs/ki/coastal_gardens-ki_v4_small.pdf)). The lesson travels: sort your garden into an exposed edge and a sheltered interior, and plant each accordingly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best windbreak for a coastal garden?

A permeable one. The RHS recommends a barrier that filters roughly half to 60 percent of the wind, such as a hedge, woven hurdles or slatted fencing, rather than a solid wall that creates eddies. Put salt-tolerant plants on the front line.

### Do salt-tolerant plants need watering?

Yes, while they establish. Extension guidance from Florida and Massachusetts stresses regular watering at the start, even for drought-tolerant plants, because roots need time to settle. After that, many need little extra.

### Can I grow tender plants in a coastal garden?

Sometimes. Mild coastal winters let you try plants that would struggle inland, and the RHS mentions bottlebrush and agave as examples. Wind and salt can still scorch them, so give them your most sheltered spot and expect some trial and error.

### What if my garden is exposed but not near the sea?

The wind advice still applies. Hilltop gardens may lack salt but are often colder and drier, so choose plants rated for lower temperatures, use a permeable windbreak, and shelter your most delicate planting.

### Should I improve sandy soil before planting?

Improve the planting holes or bed with compost or well-rotted manure rather than the whole garden. Many coastal plants like lean soil, and gravel or pebble mulch helps hold moisture.
