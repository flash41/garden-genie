  **Quick answer.** In a coastal or exposed garden, wind and salt spray usually decide what lives, often more than the winter minimum on a plant label. Filter the wind with a permeable windbreak rather than blocking it with a solid wall, put tough salt-tolerant plants on the front line, and tuck more sensitive plants into the shelter behind them.

If your garden sits on a headland, a hillside or a gust-funnelling estate edge, you may have noticed that the usual planting advice doesn't quite fit. The plant is "hardy", the soil looks fine, and yet the leaves go brown at the edges by midsummer and the whole thing leans like it's walking into a gale. This guide explains why, and how to plant so the wind works with you instead of against you.

---

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture the scenario. You move to a house a few minutes from the sea, or to a bare hilltop plot with a view and no neighbours to break the weather. You buy a handsome hydrangea and a few lovely perennials, tuck them into a border, and enjoy them for about six weeks. Then a spring gale arrives, the leaves crisp and curl, and you assume you've done something wrong.

You haven't. You've met the real constraint of an exposed site. Wind dries plants out faster than their roots can replace the water, and on the coast it often carries salt as well. Neither shows up in a hardiness rating, which is why we always say a zone number is only the start of the conversation. If you haven't looked at yours yet, our guides to [UK hardiness zones](/notes/what-planting-zone-is-the-uk-in) and [Irish hardiness zones](/notes/plant-hardiness-zones-ireland-beginners-guide) are a good place to begin, and [microclimates in your garden](/notes/microclimates-in-your-garden) explains why one corner can behave so differently from the next.

## Wind and Salt: The Two Constraints Hardiness Ratings Miss

The Royal Horticultural Society describes the main challenge of a seaside garden as blustery wind and salty sea spray that dry plants out quickly ([RHS, Coastal plants](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/articles/misc/coastal-plants)). University of Massachusetts guidance lists a similar set for the US east coast: salt spray, wind, poor soil, dry conditions and sometimes shifting sand or saltwater overwash ([UMass Extension, Selection and Maintenance of Plant Materials for Coastal Landscapes](https://www.umass.edu/agriculture-food-environment/landscape/fact-sheets/selection-maintenance-of-plant-materials-for-coastal-landscapes)).

Australian gardeners describe it in vivid terms. Salt-laden winds can leave a greasy film on leaves and windows and burn foliage, and how bad it gets depends on how close you are to the shore and which way the wind blows. A garden several streets back, or tucked behind a headland, can be far gentler ([Australian Native Plants Society, Gardening in coastal conditions](https://anpsa.org.au/APOL13/mar99-5.html)).

Three practical takeaways:

- **Exposure is not the same as cold.** Many coastal gardens enjoy mild winters, so frost-tender plants can sometimes be tried there, but the wind can still scorch them. That's the opposite of an exposed hill garden, which may be colder and windier with no salt at all.
- **Distance matters, but not in a neat line.** Florida's extension service suggests treating plants within roughly an eighth of a mile of salt water as needing at least some salt tolerance ([UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions, Salt-Tolerant Plants](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/design/landscaping-for-specific-sites/salt-tolerant-plants/)). Your own garden may be better or worse depending on the prevailing wind and the land around it.
- **Look at what's already surviving.** Both the UMass and Australian sources suggest watching which local plants thrive nearby. Hedges, hawthorns and wild coastal species that look healthy are free advice.

## Windbreaks: Filter the Wind, Don't Fight It

Here is the counterintuitive bit that saves a lot of disappointment. A solid fence or wall feels like the obvious answer to wind, but it isn't. The RHS advises that a windbreak should be semi-permeable, ideally filtering around half to 60 percent of the wind, because a solid barrier creates damaging eddies on both sides ([RHS, Windbreaks and shelterbelts](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/hedges/windbreaks-shelterbelts)). Woven willow hurdles, slatted fencing and a good hedge all do this well.

The same page says a windbreak reduces wind on its sheltered side for a distance of around ten times its height, and that it should be wide enough, or have returns at the ends, so the wind doesn't simply slip round the sides. That's a useful rule of thumb when you're deciding where a hedge or fence should go: a 1.5 metre barrier is doing useful work for a good long stretch of garden behind it.

A few design ideas we've found work well in exposed gardens:

- **Layer it.** UMass suggests low, tough plants on the exposed front line, such as rugosa rose or bayberry, with taller evergreens behind in a staggered double row, rising in height away from the wind.
- **Use a hedge instead of a wall where you can.** Australian coastal advice notes that dense hedges or shelter belts widen your plant choices dramatically, and that looking through trunks can be nicer than a blocked-off view.
- **Plant small.** The RHS recommends small, young plants for shelter belts because they establish better than large ones. Mulch them and water through the first dry spells.
- **Keep the view.** A permeable screen can frame a sea view rather than hide it. On a photo of your own garden, it's easy to see where a screen would sit without blocking the best sightline.

## Salt-Tolerant Plants, Sorted by Job

Rather than a long list, it helps to think about the job each plant does. These are examples drawn from the sources above; always check what suits your own region, and ask a local nursery which varieties they know cope with salt. The Australian guidance is blunt about this: choose plants of coastal origin, because the same species grown inland may not tolerate salt as well.

**The front line (shelter belt and hedge).** The RHS windbreak list includes *Elaeagnus × ebbingei*, yew, hawthorn, hazel and sea buckthorn, and flags hawthorn, sea buckthorn and willows as coping with salt-laden coastal winds. In the US, the UMass guide points to rugosa rose and beach plum among others, and Australian gardeners often turn to coastal natives such as pigface (*Carpobrotus*) and coastal heathland plants.

**Soft, silvery fillers.** The RHS notes that plants with narrow, grass-like or needle-like foliage, or silvery, leathery or waxy leaves, tend to cope well. That points to rosemary, sage and thyme, thrift (*Armeria*) and sea holly (*Eryngium*). UMass also lists English lavender among its non-native options.

**Structure and drama.** The RHS suggests tender exotics like bottlebrush and agave can thrive by the sea even though they struggle inland. In Florida, extension examples include cabbage palms and sea oats. If you're in a mild coastal pocket, this is where a warm, sheltered spot can pay off.

**Ground cover and grasses.** Native beach grasses and sedges (UMass), and muhly grass or gaillardia in warmer coasts (UF/IFAS) all suit sandy, windy ground. In Australia, grey foliage plants such as *Atriplex* and *Rhagodia* can give a lovely silvery "grey garden" even on shallow, limey soils.

A note of honesty: salt tolerance is a spectrum, not a yes or no. Even tough plants can look ragged in a bad gale, so expect some leaf scorch and plan for a front line that is easy to trim or replace.

## Sandy, Free-Draining Soil: Help Plants Settle In

Seaside soil is often sandy or stony and drains quickly. The RHS says many seaside plants actually prefer these poor conditions and stay healthier there than in rich inland soil. Our companion piece on [clay versus sandy soil](/notes/clay-soil-vs-sandy-soil-what-will-grow) goes into this in more detail.

What helps in practice:

- **Improve the planting area, not the whole garden.** UMass recommends working in a generous layer of organic matter such as compost or well-rotted manure to help sandy soil hold nutrients and moisture. The RHS says compost can help establishment too.
- **Mulch with gravel or pebbles.** The RHS suggests locally sourced pebbles and gravel as a mulch to hold moisture and suppress weeds, and it looks right in the setting.
- **Water the first season.** UF/IFAS puts it neatly: even with salt water all around, coastal plants need fresh water to thrive. Drought-tolerant plants still need watering until they've rooted in. Our guide to [drought-tolerant planting](/notes/drought-tolerant-water-wise-planting) covers the longer-term approach.

## Planting Low, Staking Smart and Choosing Hard Landscaping

Exposed gardens reward a lower profile. Put the tallest, most wind-sensitive plants behind a hedge or wall, keep the front of the garden in mounds, mats and clipped shapes, and leave the open space to tough grasses.

- **Protect new plants.** The RHS suggests shielding new plants from wind while they get established. Simple pebble or driftwood mini-windbreaks, or a temporary screen of netting, can make the difference in year one.
- **Stake with restraint.** Staking can help a young tree through its first seasons, but a tree that is held rigid never builds its own strength. We've found it's better to use a short, low stake and remove it once the roots have taken hold.
- **Choose hard landscaping for the weather.** In our experience, fixings and furniture near the sea tend to corrode, so favour stainless or well-protected metals, masonry, timber suited to outdoor use and gravel surfaces. Check with your supplier what they recommend for salty air. Low, solid walls can create shelter pockets for seating, even if a tall solid wall causes turbulence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best windbreak for a coastal garden?

A permeable one. The RHS recommends a barrier that filters roughly half to 60 percent of the wind, such as a hedge, woven hurdles or slatted fencing, rather than a solid wall that creates eddies. Choose salt-tolerant plants on the front line.

### Do salt-tolerant plants need watering?

Yes, while they establish. Extension guidance from Florida and Massachusetts both stresses regular watering at the start, even for drought-tolerant plants, because roots need time to settle. After that, many need little extra.

### Can I grow tender plants in a coastal garden?

Sometimes. Mild coastal winters let you try plants that would struggle inland, and the RHS mentions bottlebrush and agave as examples. Wind and salt can still scorch them, so give them the most sheltered spot you have and expect some trial and error.

### What if my garden is exposed but not near the sea?

The wind advice still applies. Hilltop gardens may lack salt but are often colder and drier, so choose plants rated for lower temperatures, use a permeable windbreak, and shelter the most delicate planting.

### Should I improve sandy soil before planting?

Improve the planting holes or bed with compost or well-rotted manure rather than the whole garden. Many coastal plants like lean soil, and mulching with gravel or pebbles helps hold moisture.
