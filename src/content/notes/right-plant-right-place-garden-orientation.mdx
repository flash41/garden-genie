"Right plant, right place" is one of the oldest bits of advice in gardening, and one of the easiest to nod along to and then ignore at the garden centre. The catch is that "right place" has a concrete starting point: which way your garden faces, and the light that brings.

---

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture the familiar scene. You buy three lovely shrubs on a bright Saturday, plant them along the back fence, and by midsummer they are leggy, scorched or sulking. Nothing was wrong with the plants, and you probably watered them faithfully. The problem is that the back fence sits in shade until mid-afternoon, or bakes against a wall from noon onwards, and the plants you chose wanted the opposite.

Plants in the wrong spot struggle; plants that match their conditions tend to get on with it. The Royal Horticultural Society's advice is to work with what your garden already offers instead of trying to change it completely, and aspect is the biggest single thing your garden offers.

---

## What "Aspect" Actually Means

Your garden's aspect is simply the direction it faces. Gardeners usually describe it from the house looking out, so a "south-facing garden" is one where, standing at the back door, you look south into the garden. Beds and borders within that garden can have their own aspects too: the border against the side fence may face a different way from the lawn.

In the northern hemisphere, the sun tracks across the southern half of the sky. That gives you a simple rule of thumb, which the [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-inspiration/design/get-to-know-your-garden) puts neatly: south- and west-facing aspects are usually warm and sunny, while east- and north-facing aspects are colder and shadier. East gets the morning sun, west gets the afternoon and evening sun.

**A note for Australian readers.** Everything above flips for you. In the southern hemisphere the sun tracks across the northern half of the sky, so a **north-facing** garden or wall is the sunny, warm one, and a **south-facing** one is the shadier, cooler one. East and west still mean morning and afternoon sun respectively. Southern-hemisphere design guidance also notes that the winter sun sits lower in the sky and further north, which changes how far shadows reach in the cooler months ([Glenice Buck Designs](https://www.hilltopsphoenix.com.au/practical-garden-hints-for-designing-your-garden-which-way-is-north)).

To keep this note useful wherever you are, from here on we will talk about **the sunny side** and **the shady side** of your garden, and let you work out which compass direction that is for you.

---

## How Sun and Shade Behave Through the Day and the Year

Aspect is only half the story. The other half is time.

**Across the day.** An east-facing bed catches gentle morning light, then falls into shade. A west-facing bed does the reverse: cool in the morning, then strong afternoon and evening sun, which is often the hottest of the day. A bed on the sunny side can get light for most of the day, while one on the shady side may never see direct sun in the parts of the garden close to a wall.

**Across the seasons.** The sun sits lower in winter, so shadows from walls, fences and buildings stretch much further. A border that is bright in June can be in shade for much of December. Deciduous trees add another layer: the shade beneath them is dappled in summer but, as Gardeners' World describes it, closer to full sun from autumn to spring, which is exactly why woodland plants that flower early do so well there ([Gardeners' World](https://tollbit.gardenersworld.com/plants/types-of-garden-shade)).

**Light is a spectrum, not a switch.** It helps to think in terms like:

- **Full sun:** direct sun for most of the day.
- **Partial shade:** a few hours of direct summer sun, then shade (definitions vary between sources).
- **Dappled shade:** moving, filtered light beneath trees.
- **Deep shade:** little or no direct sun, such as on the shady side of a wall or beneath dense evergreens.

Plant labels use these same ideas, so our guide to [plant label symbols](/notes/what-do-plant-label-symbols-mean) pairs well with your map.

## What Tends to Thrive in Each Aspect

These are starting points, not guarantees. Soil, shelter and your local climate all matter too, and we would always suggest checking a plant's own requirements. The examples below follow the northern-hemisphere pattern; Australian readers can swap "north" and "south".

**The sunny, warm side (south and west in the northern hemisphere).** This is where heat-lovers earn their place. The RHS notes that the extra warmth of a south wall can allow plants such as peaches and passionflowers that would struggle elsewhere. Think Mediterranean-style planting, lavender, sun-loving herbs, and fruit trained against a wall. West-facing spots suit sturdier plants that cope with strong afternoon heat, and Gardeners' World gives magnolias and camellias as examples, though they will want shelter and the right soil.

**The morning-sun side (east).** Plants that enjoy morning light but dislike scorching afternoon sun do well here. Gardeners' World suggests clematis and honeysuckle for east-facing spots.

**The shady side (north and, to a lesser extent, east).** Shade is not a problem to be solved, it is a different set of plants. Ferns, hostas and ivy suit deep shade, while hellebores are a classic choice for shadier spots because they flower before the canopy and summer shade close in. Partial shade suits plants such as alchemilla and hardy geraniums. We cover this aspect in depth in [what will grow in a north-facing garden](/notes/what-will-grow-in-a-north-facing-garden).

## How Aspect Interacts With Walls, Trees and Local Climate

A compass bearing gives you the general picture. Your actual garden adds the details.

- **Walls and fences** cast long shadows on their shady side but also store and reflect heat on their sunny side, creating a warmer pocket. That is the heart of a microclimate, which we explore in [microclimates in your garden](/notes/microclimates-in-your-garden).
- **Neighbouring buildings and trees** can turn a "sunny" aspect into a half-shaded one. Always trust what you observe over what the compass suggests.
- **Your regional climate** sets the outer limits for what survives winter at all. Aspect fine-tunes within those limits. If you have not yet checked your hardiness rating, start with our guides for [Ireland](/notes/plant-hardiness-zones-ireland-beginners-guide) and [the UK](/notes/what-planting-zone-is-the-uk-in), then use aspect to pick between the plants that pass that first test.

## Map Your Own Garden: A Step-by-Step Guide

This is the practical part. It takes a little patience, but it is the most useful hour (or several short check-ins) you can spend before buying a single plant.

1. **Find north.** Most smartphones have a compass tool, as the RHS points out, so stand at your back door and check which way you are facing. Write down the bearing, then note which way each border, fence and wall faces too. Remember to allow for the hemisphere you are in when deciding which direction is the sunny one.
2. **Sketch a rough plan.** On paper, draw the outline of your garden with the house, fences, sheds, large trees and anything tall that casts a shadow. Mark north on it. It does not need to be to scale.
3. **Check the light at several times of day.** Gardening Know How suggests marking where sun and shade fall every hour, or every two hours if that is more realistic, from morning to evening, and colour-coding the result ([Gardening Know How](https://www.gardeningknowhow.com/garden-how-to/info/making-a-sun-map.htm)). Even three snapshots (morning, midday, late afternoon) will teach you a lot.
4. **Do it more than once.** The same source suggests spring and midsummer are the best times, and if you can only manage one session, choose midsummer. If you can, add a quick check in winter to see how far the shadows really reach when the sun is low.
5. **Label each area.** Using the categories above, mark zones on your plan as full sun, partial shade, dappled shade or deep shade. Add notes on wind, dryness near walls and any wet spots.
6. **Match plants to zones.** Only now start building a plant list. Treat each zone as its own small brief, and read plant labels for light requirements.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I find out which way my garden faces?
Stand at your back door looking out and use the compass on your smartphone. Whichever direction you are facing is the direction your garden faces. Then repeat the check for individual borders, as a side fence or a corner bed may face a different way from the main lawn.

### Which direction is best for a garden?
There is no single best aspect. A sunny side suits sun-loving plants, vegetables and seating areas, while a shadier side suits woodland-style planting and gives welcome relief on hot days. What matters is choosing plants that suit the light you have.

### Is a north-facing garden the shady one everywhere?
Not everywhere. In the northern hemisphere (UK, Ireland, US) north-facing is typically the shadier aspect. In the southern hemisphere, including Australia, north-facing is the sunnier, warmer side, and south-facing is the shadier one.

### Can I grow sun-loving plants in a shady garden?
Usually with difficulty. Sun-loving plants in too little light tend to grow leggy and flower poorly. It is generally easier to look for plants that enjoy partial or dappled shade, and to place any sun-lovers in the brightest spot you can find, such as against a sunny wall.

### Does my aspect change through the year?
Your garden always faces the same way, but the sun's path changes. In winter the sun is lower, so shadows from walls, fences and trees stretch further, and parts of the garden that are bright in summer can be shaded for much of winter.
