  **Quick answer.** Put sun-lovers (lavender, herbs, fruit trained on a wall) on the south- and west-facing sides, plant shade-tolerant foliage and woodland flowers (ferns, hostas, hellebores) on the north- and east-facing sides, and choose morning-sun climbers such as clematis for the east. "Right plant, right place" is one of the oldest bits of gardening advice, and one of the easiest to nod along to and then forget at the garden centre. The catch is that "right place" has a concrete starting point: which way your garden faces, and the light that brings.

---

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Picture the familiar scene. You buy three lovely shrubs on a bright Saturday, plant them along the back fence, and by midsummer they are leggy, scorched or sulking. Nothing was wrong with the plants, and you probably watered them faithfully. The problem is that the fence sits in shade until mid-afternoon, or bakes against a wall from noon onwards, and the plants you chose wanted the opposite.

The Royal Horticultural Society advises working with what your garden already offers rather than trying to change it completely, and aspect is the biggest single thing your garden offers.

---

## What Does "Aspect" Actually Mean?

Aspect is simply the direction a garden, wall or border faces. Gardeners usually describe it from the house looking out, so a "south-facing garden" is one where, standing at the back door, you look south. Beds within that garden have their own aspects too: the border against the side fence may face a different way from the lawn.

In the northern hemisphere the sun tracks across the southern half of the sky. The [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-inspiration/design/get-to-know-your-garden) sums up the rule of thumb: south- and west-facing aspects are usually warm and sunny, while east- and north-facing aspects are colder and shadier. East gets the morning sun, west the afternoon and evening sun.

In the southern hemisphere the sun tracks across the northern half of the sky, so the sunny and shady sides swap. We cover that in the regional section below.

---

## What Should You Plant on the Sunny, Warm Side?

This is where heat-lovers earn their place. The RHS notes that the extra warmth of a south-facing wall can let you grow plants such as peaches and passionflowers that would struggle elsewhere. Think Mediterranean-style planting, lavender, sun-loving herbs and fruit trained against a wall. The RHS also explains that a south-facing wall warms up in the day and releases that warmth overnight, which helps fruit ripen and takes the edge off frost ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/profile?pid=689)).

West-facing spots suit sturdier plants that cope with strong afternoon heat. Gardeners' World gives magnolias and camellias as examples, though they will want shelter and the right soil.

One practical warning: walls create a rain shadow, so the soil right at their base, up to about a metre out according to the RHS, can be very dry. Water new plants there generously for the first season.

---

## What Grows Best in Morning Sun (East-Facing)?

Plants that enjoy gentle morning light but dislike scorching afternoon sun do well here. Gardeners' World suggests clematis and honeysuckle for east-facing spots. East-facing beds also cool down early, which suits plants that sulk in heat. The trade-off is that cold mornings after frost can be harsh on tender growth, because the sun reaches frosted leaves quickly, so keep your most frost-tender plants elsewhere.

---

## What Grows in Shade (North-Facing)?

Shade is not a problem to be solved; it is a different set of plants. Ferns, hostas and ivy suit deep shade. Hellebores are a classic for shadier spots because they flower before the canopy and summer shade close in, and partial shade suits alchemilla and hardy geraniums. The RHS adds that the north side of a wall or fence has a more even climate without extremes, and suits hardy fuchsias, hellebores and Japanese anemones.

For a wall, the RHS's award-winning picks for cold, dark north-facing walls include climbing rose 'Madame Alfred Carrière', climbing hydrangea (*Hydrangea petiolaris*), *Osmanthus delavayi* and variegated ivy ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/articles/graham-rice/10-agm-plants-for-cold-walls)). We go deeper in [what will grow in a north-facing garden](/notes/what-will-grow-in-a-north-facing-garden).

## How Do the Day, the Seasons and Trees Change the Picture?

A compass bearing gives you the general picture; time and surroundings add the detail.

**Across the day.** An east-facing bed catches morning light then falls into shade; a west-facing bed does the reverse, with the hottest sun arriving in the afternoon.

**Across the seasons.** The sun sits lower in winter, so shadows from walls, fences and buildings stretch much further. A border that is bright in June can be shaded for much of December. Deciduous trees add another layer: the shade beneath them is dappled in summer but closer to full sun from autumn to spring, as [Gardeners' World](https://tollbit.gardenersworld.com/plants/types-of-garden-shade) describes, which is why early-flowering woodland plants do so well there.

**Light is a spectrum.** Think in four levels: full sun (direct sun most of the day), partial shade (a few hours of direct summer sun), dappled shade (moving, filtered light under trees) and deep shade (little or no direct sun). Plant labels use the same ideas, so our guide to [plant label symbols](/notes/what-do-plant-label-symbols-mean) pairs well with your map.

**Neighbouring buildings and trees** can turn a "sunny" aspect into a half-shaded one. Always trust what you observe over what the compass says. That small-scale effect is the heart of a microclimate, which we explore in [microclimates in your garden](/notes/microclimates-in-your-garden).

## How Do You Map the Sun in Your Own Garden?

It takes a little patience, but this is the most useful hour (or several short check-ins) you can spend before buying a plant.

1. **Find north.** Most smartphones have a compass tool. Stand at your back door, note which way you face, then check each border, fence and wall too.
2. **Sketch a rough plan.** Draw the garden outline with the house, fences, sheds and large trees, and mark north. It need not be to scale.
3. **Check the light at several times of day.** Gardening Know How suggests marking where sun and shade fall every hour or two from morning to evening, colour-coding the result ([Gardening Know How](https://www.gardeningknowhow.com/garden-how-to/info/making-a-sun-map.htm)). Even three snapshots (morning, midday, late afternoon) teach you a lot.
4. **Do it more than once.** The same source suggests spring and midsummer are the best times, and midsummer if you only manage one session. A winter check shows how far shadows really reach.
5. **Label each area** as full sun, partial shade, dappled shade or deep shade, with notes on wind and dry spots.
6. **Match plants to zones.** Only now build a plant list, treating each zone as its own small brief.

## By region

The compass rule is the same across the northern hemisphere, but how much it matters, and what you can grow, changes with latitude and climate.

### UK

The UK spans roughly 50 to 60 degrees north, and that makes north-facing gardens noticeably different from one end of the country to the other. On the winter solstice the midday sun reaches only about 15 degrees above the horizon in London, around 12 degrees in Edinburgh and about 8 degrees in Shetland, compared with about 62 degrees at midsummer in London ([Diamond Geezer](https://diamondgeezer.blogspot.com/2017/12/solar-elevation.html)). Those are approximate figures from a blog post rather than an official source, but they show the pattern: the further north you are, the longer winter shadows become.

In practice, a north-facing garden in southern England and one in Scotland both lose direct sun on the ground for much of the winter, but the Scottish one loses it for longer and the shadows from a house or tall fence reach further. One UK sun guide estimates that a two-storey house casts a shadow around 30 metres long at midwinter noon in southern England, and longer further north ([Solpath](https://solpath.co.uk/guides/garden-orientation-and-sunlight.php)). That is a single non-official source, so treat it as a rough guide. Our advice: in the north of the UK, assume the shady side stays shady all winter and choose reliably shade-tolerant plants, and give your best sunny spot to anything that needs ripening or flowering heat.

### Ireland

The same orientation rule applies, and Irish gardens sit within the broader UK-and-Ireland latitude band, roughly 51 to 55 degrees north, so winter shadows are similar to those of northern England. One Irish gardening columnist notes that Irish gardeners often shy away from shade because we are in semi-shade much of the time under grey skies, yet shade has real value, and suggests lungwort, hardy geranium 'Joan Baker', royal fern and periwinkle for it ([Irish News](https://www.irishnews.com/lifestyle/2017/06/17/news/the-casual-gardener-even-in-ireland-we-need-to-take-shade-into-account-1057267/)). The takeaway for Irish gardens: use your sunniest wall for the plants that need warmth, and do not be afraid to plant boldly in the shade, especially where soil is damp.

### US

Aspect works the same way across the US, with the sunny, warm side facing south. A Washington State University extension master gardeners' note describes south-facing slopes and walls getting more direct sun and staying warmer for longer, with a south-facing wall suiting climbing roses and clematis, and moist, mostly shady spots suiting ferns ([WSU Skagit County Extension](https://wpcdn.web.wsu.edu/extension/uploads/sites/57/2019/06/Microclimates.pdf)). The same source stresses choosing and placing plants for the microclimates in your own yard rather than relying only on USDA hardiness zones. Check your zone first (our note on [RHS hardiness versus USDA zones](/notes/rhs-hardiness-vs-usda-zones) explains how the systems differ), then use aspect to choose among plants that pass that test.

### Australia

Everything flips. In the southern hemisphere the sun tracks across the northern half of the sky, so north-facing is the sunny, warm aspect and south-facing the shadier, cooler one. East and west still mean morning and afternoon sun. Gardening Australia's advice for a vegetable patch is to choose an open aspect, as north-facing as possible, so crops get light from early morning to late afternoon ([Gardening Australia, ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/gardening/how-to/faqs-vegie-patch-sunlight-spacing-for-hedging/9440504)). Winter shadows are longer here too, so Gardening Australia suggests putting trellises and taller plants on the south side so they do not shade the rest of your crops ([Gardening Australia, ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/gardening/how-to/a-shady-patch/9433782)). In a courtyard, Gardening Australia notes that north-facing areas may get sun most of the day, while east-facing ones might only get it in the morning ([Gardening Australia, ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/gardening/factsheets/designing-a-courtyard-garden/9431190)). If you are reading northern-hemisphere plant advice, swap "south" for "north" and vice versa.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I find out which way my garden faces?
Stand at your back door looking out and use the compass on your smartphone. The direction you face is the direction your garden faces. Then check individual borders, as a side fence or corner bed may face a different way from the main lawn.

### Which direction is best for a garden?
South-facing is best in the northern hemisphere (north-facing in Australia) if you want the widest choice of plants, vegetables and seating in sun. But no aspect is bad: a shadier side suits woodland-style planting and gives welcome relief on hot days.

### Is a north-facing garden always the shady one?
No. In the UK, Ireland and the US, north-facing is typically the shadier aspect. In Australia and the rest of the southern hemisphere, north-facing is the sunnier, warmer side and south-facing is the shadier.

### Can I grow sun-loving plants in a shady garden?
Not well. Sun-lovers in too little light tend to grow leggy and flower poorly. Choose plants for partial or dappled shade, and put any sun-lovers in the brightest spot you have, such as against the sunniest wall.

### Does my aspect change through the year?
Your garden always faces the same way, but the sun's path changes. In winter the sun is lower, so shadows from walls, fences and trees stretch further, and parts that are bright in summer can be shaded for much of winter.
