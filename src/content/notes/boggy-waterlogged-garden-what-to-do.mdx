A waterlogged garden rarely announces itself in summer. It shows up in October, when the first run of proper rain arrives and the lawn starts to squelch underfoot. Puddles that used to clear by lunchtime are still there the next morning. The border by the fence has turned to sludge, and a shrub you planted in spring is yellowing for no obvious reason.

---

## Why This Matters in Your Back Garden

Most plants need air in the soil as well as water. When soil stays saturated, the air is pushed out, and roots struggle. The Royal Horticultural Society (RHS) notes that waterlogged soil is often compacted or naturally dense, and that damage tends to be worse in the growing season, when roots need more oxygen, than in cold winter conditions. In other words, a spot that looks merely "a bit damp" in winter can quietly kill plants in spring and summer.

The good news is that wet gardens are very workable. Some fixes cost nothing but a change of habit, and there is a whole palette of beautiful plants that actively prefer these conditions. The trick is working out what is causing your problem before you reach for a spade or a contractor.

---

## Why Gardens Waterlog

It helps to know which of these is behind your problem, because the fixes differ.

- **Compaction.** Heavy foot traffic, building work, machinery and digging when the soil is wet all squeeze out the pores that let water drain. University extension guidance from Illinois points out that working soil while saturated can make things worse, creating dense layers that block drainage.
- **Clay or dense soil.** Clay has very small pores, so water moves through it slowly.
- **A high water table.** In low-lying land, or near a river or spring, groundwater can sit close to the surface for much of the winter.
- **Runoff and hard surfaces.** The RHS points out that hard landscaping reduces the soil area available to absorb rain, concentrating water in smaller zones. A new patio, driveway or extension can change where water goes.
- **Low spots.** Water simply runs downhill and collects where the ground dips, whether in your garden or from a neighbour's.

Often it is a combination. A compacted layer of clay at the bottom of a hollow is a classic recipe for a bog.

## How to Test Your Drainage Before You Spend Anything

A simple hole test tells you far more than guessing. The method below follows guidance from Iowa State University Extension, which we've adapted to metric.

1. Dig a hole roughly 30cm (12in) deep and 10 to 30cm wide.
2. Fill it with water and let it drain away completely. This pre-wets the soil.
3. Fill it again straight away and note the water level.
4. After 15 minutes, measure how far the level has dropped, and multiply by four to estimate the drop per hour.

Iowa State describes a drop of roughly 2.5 to 7.5cm (1 to 3in) an hour as desirable for most plants, and less than about 2.5cm (1in) an hour as poor drainage. Treat these as rough guides rather than exact thresholds.

Do the test in a few places, and again after a wet spell, because one spot can behave very differently from another. Dig a second, deeper hole in your worst area too. If water seeps in from the sides or the bottom, that points towards a high water table rather than just dense topsoil. Take photos and note where water lies after rain. That record becomes extremely useful later, whether you are planning a layout yourself or briefing a professional.

## Fixes, From Least to Most Effort

### 1. Stop making it worse

Keep off saturated soil and lawn where you can, and avoid digging or rotovating when it is wet. Use boards or stepping stones on regular routes. It costs nothing and prevents further compaction.

### 2. Improve the soil structure

Both the RHS and university extension services recommend adding organic matter. Iowa State suggests working a few inches of compost into the top 15 to 30cm of the soil to improve structure and pore space, and Illinois Extension notes that aeration or tillage can break up hardpans. Do this when the soil is moist rather than sodden. It helps with compaction and moderately heavy ground, but it won't rescue a plot sitting on a high water table.

### 3. Plant for the conditions

Often the most cost-effective answer is to stop fighting the wetness. See the plant section below.

### 4. Raised beds and mounds

When you can't change the ground, change the level. The RHS suggests raised beds as an effective option for poorly draining sites. Lifting roots above the wettest soil gives them air, and it is a very approachable DIY job. Be aware that a raised bed sitting on a permanently saturated base can still be wet at the bottom, so choose plants accordingly.

### 5. Rain gardens, swales and seasonal ponds

If water collects at a low point anyway, give it somewhere useful to go. The RHS lists seasonal ponds and rain gardens at the garden's low points among its drainage options, and Iowa State also suggests redirecting surface water through rain gardens or reshaping depressions. A shallow planted dip, or a gentle ditch-like swale that carries water to it, holds rain for a while and lets it soak away slowly. These suit gardens where the issue is surface water rather than a permanently high water table.

### 6. Drains and soakaways

Land drains, French drains and soakaways can move large volumes of water, but they are the point where this becomes structural work. They need the right fall, a suitable place for the water to end up, and soil that can absorb it. A badly sited soakaway can send water to a neighbour's land or simply fill up and fail. We'd strongly suggest professional advice here. If flooding appears to come from an overflowing public drain, the RHS suggests contacting your water company.

## When to Call a Professional

Consider getting advice if water stays on the surface for days, if it reaches your house or outbuildings, if you suspect a spring or high groundwater, or if the fix involves digging trenches or channelling water off your property. In some places, planning, building or water regulations also apply to drainage and to surfacing over a front garden, so check what applies locally. For smaller problems, a mix of better soil care, raised beds and the right plants is often enough, and we've found that matching the solution to the real cause saves a lot of wasted effort.

## Plants That Tolerate Wet Soil

A useful distinction: some plants like moist soil, some tolerate seasonal waterlogging, and a few cope with standing water. Most "bog garden" plants like wet soil but not swimming in water, as the RHS puts it, so a spot that is under water for long periods may need a more aquatic approach.

The RHS bog garden list includes, among many others:

- **Perennials:** astilbe, rodgersia, ligularia, hostas, Siberian and yellow flag iris (*Iris sibirica*, *Iris pseudacorus*), candelabra primulas (*Primula japonica*, *P. florindae*), meadowsweet (*Filipendula ulmaria*) and marsh marigold (*Caltha palustris*).
- **Grasses and sedge-like plants:** *Carex elata* 'Aurea' and soft rush (*Juncus effusus*).
- **Ferns:** royal fern (*Osmunda regalis*), ostrich fern (*Matteuccia struthiopteris*) and male fern.
- **Trees:** the RHS mentions willows for persistently wet areas, and Iowa State names bald cypress as a tree for wet soils.

For wetter-then-drier ground, the RHS has a separate list for seasonally wet and dry soils, and notes that not every "suitable" plant works in every garden, so some experimenting is realistic. Check hardiness for your own climate too: the RHS H-ratings, USDA zones and Australian equivalents all matter alongside moisture. Our [RHS and USDA comparison](/notes/rhs-hardiness-vs-usda-zones) explains how to read them.

## Planning Around the Wet Spots

The most helpful thing you can do before changing anything is map where the water goes. Put the wettest areas on a plan, then decide what lives where: a rain garden or moisture-loving border in the hollow, raised beds for plants that need better drainage, and paths and seating on the driest ground.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is my garden boggy in winter but fine in summer?

Rainfall is higher and plants and evaporation take up less water in winter, so a high water table or slow-draining soil shows itself. Summer dries it out, which is why a test hole after a wet spell is more revealing than one in August.

### How do I test whether my garden drains well?

Dig a hole about 30cm deep, fill it, let it empty, refill it and measure the drop after 15 minutes. Multiply by four for a rough hourly rate. Around 2.5 to 7.5cm an hour is considered good for most plants, and under about 2.5cm is poor.

### Will adding compost or sand fix waterlogged soil?

Organic matter such as compost improves structure and is widely recommended. Sand is less reliable on clay, because in the wrong proportions it can make the soil more like concrete. Neither will solve a high water table or heavy runoff on its own.

### Do I need a soakaway?

Only if simpler measures haven't worked and the soil beneath can actually absorb the water. This is structural work, so we'd suggest professional advice before spending money on one.

### What can I plant in a permanently wet area?

Bog and marginal plants such as iris, astilbe, rodgersia, ligularia, royal fern and meadowsweet suit moist soil, and willows cope with persistently wet ground. Avoid plants that need sharp drainage unless they go on a raised bed.
