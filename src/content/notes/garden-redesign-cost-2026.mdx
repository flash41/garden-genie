It is one of the first questions anyone asks when they start thinking seriously about their garden, and it is also one of the hardest to answer well. Type it into a search engine and you will get a wall of figures with enormous ranges and a lot of hedging. "It depends on the size of the project." Yes. Thank you. Very helpful.

So let us try to do better than that. What follows is an honest breakdown of what a garden redesign actually costs in Ireland in 2026 — what drives the price up, what brings it down, what the numbers typically look like at different levels of ambition, and where people most often get caught off guard. There is no magic number, but there are patterns worth understanding before you talk to anyone.

---

## First: Understand What You Are Actually Pricing

A "garden redesign" is not a single thing. It is a sequence of stages, each with its own cost, and not everyone needs all of them. Getting confused about which stage you are budgeting for is how people end up with a quote that does not match their expectations.

The stages, as outlined by [Houzz Ireland's landscaping guide](https://www.houzz.ie/magazine/how-much-would-it-cost-to-redesign-my-garden-stsetivw-vs~127833468), typically look like this: an initial consultation (often free, sometimes charged at the designer's hourly rate), a survey and site analysis, a concept design, a masterplan with full material specification and planting plan, then construction — the hard landscaping — and finally planting.

Most of the large numbers you will see quoted online are for the construction and planting stages. The design work is typically a separate line item, and it is the stage that most self-implementing gardeners need to think about more carefully than they do.

---

## What Does Design Alone Cost?

If you want a professional garden design — a proper plan you can work from yourself, or hand to a contractor — that is a distinct service from the building work. [The Irish League of Credit Unions](https://www.creditunion.ie/blog/the-complete-cost-of-a-garden-renovation/) found that design fees typically sit at 8% to 20% of the total project cost, depending on scope and designer.

For a more concrete sense of scale, standalone design prices in Dublin have historically run at around €650 for a small garden (50–100m²), €750 for an average garden (100–250m²), €895 for a large garden (250–500m²), and €995 for an extra-large garden up to 1,000m². These are design-only figures — they cover the plan, not the implementation. They give you a useful baseline for the conversation, but actual rates in 2026 will depend on the designer and location.

---

## What Does a Full Redesign and Build Cost?

This is where the numbers get more significant. For a complete redesign — design, construction, and planting combined — the ranges in Ireland break down roughly as follows.

Small gardens and front gardens typically land between €4,000 and €8,000 for the full project, though this shifts considerably depending on materials and groundwork. For a mid-sized suburban back garden with lawn, raised beds, borders, and a patio, [one Dublin-based garden designer cited by the Irish League of Credit Unions](https://www.creditunion.ie/blog/the-complete-cost-of-a-garden-renovation/) quoted a recent project at €10,500 all-in — a useful anchor point for that level of scope. The broader range for an average suburban garden sits at €8,000–€14,000.

[Eden Landscaping's 2025 pricing guide](https://www.edenlandscaping.ie/post/planning-a-garden-makeover-here-s-how-much-you-can-expect-to-pay) puts a full medium-sized makeover in the €10,000–€25,000 range, with the upper end reflecting premium materials, significant groundwork, or features like composite decking, outdoor lighting, retaining walls, or water elements. Individual elements add up faster than most people expect: patio installation runs €2,500–€5,500 for 20m², composite decking €4,000–€7,000, artificial grass €3,500–€6,500 for 50m², and retaining walls or raised beds €2,500–€6,000.

## What Drives the Price Up?

Understanding the cost levers is more useful than any single figure, because the same size garden can cost €8,000 or €20,000 depending on choices made at the design stage.

Materials are consistently the biggest variable. [As Eden Landscaping notes](https://www.edenlandscaping.ie/post/planning-a-garden-makeover-here-s-how-much-you-can-expect-to-pay), the gap between Indian sandstone, porcelain tiles, and composite decking is significant — not just in purchase price but in laying costs, longevity, and maintenance requirements over time.

Site preparation is where surprises tend to live. If the ground needs levelling, excavation, drainage work, or removal of existing hard landscaping, those costs arrive before a single plant goes in the ground. Poor drainage — very common in Irish gardens — can add meaningfully to the total. Complexity of design matters too: a clean rectangular garden with simple planting is significantly cheaper to execute than one with curved beds, mixed levels, or structural features. And [Garden Maintenance Ireland's research](https://gardenmaintenance.ie/how-much-does-a-gardener-charge-in-ireland/) is clear that Dublin and South Dublin rates are consistently above the national average, reflecting demand and labour costs.

---

## What Brings It Down?

Phasing the project is the most effective lever most homeowners have. Few people have €15,000 ready to spend on a garden. Most projects that look like a single transformation are actually delivered in phases — hardscaping one year, planting the next, lighting the year after. Designing the whole thing properly upfront and building it in stages is one of the smartest ways to manage cost without compromising the end result.

DIY implementation makes a significant difference where it is practical. [Angi's landscaping cost research](https://www.angi.com/articles/how-much-does-landscaping-cost.htm) notes that labour accounts for 40% to 65% of total project cost. For straightforward planting work, removing yourself from that equation has a real impact on the total — but only if you have a design detailed enough to work from. Getting multiple quotes also matters. [Angi recommends interviewing at least three professionals](https://www.angi.com/articles/7-questions-ask-hiring-landscaper.htm) to understand the market and identify outliers. A quote that is significantly lower than two others usually means something is missing from the scope, the materials specification, or the contractor's insurance situation.

## The Design Cost Is the Smart Investment

Here is a perspective that does not get said enough: the design is the cheapest part of a garden project, and it is the part that determines the quality of everything else.

A proper design — with plant species matched to your soil and aspect, materials specified for your climate, implementation phased to your budget, and a clear guide to execution — is the difference between a garden that looks intentional and one that looks like a series of independent decisions made at the garden centre on a Saturday afternoon. Without it, you are making it up as you go. Which is fine for some people and genuinely frustrating for others.

The cost of getting a design right, relative to the lifetime cost of the garden, is small. The cost of getting it wrong — wrong plants in wrong positions, paving that does not drain, structures that need to be rebuilt — is not.

---

## A Realistic Budget Framework

If you are pulling together a number for your project, here is a simple way to think about it. Design-only for a self-implementing gardener runs €650–€1,500 depending on garden size and designer. A small garden full build is typically €6,000–€10,000. An average suburban garden full build runs €10,000–€18,000. A garden with significant features — composite decking, lighting, water elements — should be budgeted at €15,000–€30,000 or more.

Add 10–15% contingency to any project quote. Groundwork almost always surfaces surprises. And if those figures are above your current budget, work backwards. Design the whole thing properly now. Build what you can afford, in the right order — hard landscaping before planting, structure before softening. A good design holds its logic even when built over several years.
